# Finding Potential Clients

Module 11.1 showed that most early hours go on finding work, and Module 11.3 showed how to find the problem once you are in the room. This module is about getting into the room: deciding exactly who you serve, choosing where to look for them, and contacting them in a way that earns replies rather than annoyance or legal trouble. The guiding idea is **focus**: a clear niche makes every channel work better, because you know who to look for, what to say, and which examples to show.

Survey and platform figures are from the sources cited, at the time of writing (November 2026). Response rates in the lab are illustrative. The legal summaries are general orientation for the US and UK, not legal advice.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### Fishing with the right bait in the right pond

A specialist joiner does not stand in the high street shouting "I make wooden things!". They know their customers, say homeowners renovating period properties within twenty miles, know where those people look, architects, heritage forums, a local builders' merchant, and speak their language: sash windows, not "woodworking solutions". Finding clients for AI work is the same: a precise **ideal client profile**, the right **channels**, and **messages** about the client's problem, not your tools.

```text
 IDEAL CLIENT PROFILE (who)            CHANNELS (where)                  MESSAGE (what)
 industry: joinery, kitchen fitting    network & referrals (warm)        their problem, in their words
 size: 5 to 50 staff                   outbound email / LinkedIn         one specific result you can show
 pain signals: slow quotes,            content: case studies, posts      one easy question as the ask
   hiring an estimator                 marketplaces: Upwork, Fiverr      short, specific, no buzzwords
 budget: can pay GBP 2,000+            communities & events              follow up 2 or 3 times, politely

 focus  ->  more relevant prospects  ->  higher reply rates  ->  fewer contacts per client
```

### Why niches feel risky but are not

Choosing a niche feels like turning work away. In practice, generalists compete on price with everyone, while specialists are easier to find, easier to refer, and easier to trust, because they show examples from the prospect's own world. You can still accept work outside the niche; you just do not market for it.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### The ideal client profile

An **ideal client profile**, ICP, describes the organisations you serve best: industry, size, location if relevant, **pain signals** that suggest the problem you solve, such as job adverts for an estimator, reviews complaining about slow quotes, or a busy enquiry form, and the ability to pay. Scoring prospects against it turns a long list into a short one:

$$\text{score} = 3\,[\text{industry}] + 2\,[\text{size}] + 2 \min(\text{signals}, 2) + 2\,[\text{budget}]$$

where each bracket is 1 when the prospect matches. A score of 11 is a perfect match; low scores are not contacted.

### Choosing a niche

A niche is attractive when its problem is **painful**, its businesses can **pay**, you can **reach** them, and **competition** for them is modest. A simple multiplicative score keeps any very weak factor from being hidden by strong ones:

$$\text{niche score} = \frac{\text{pain} \times \text{ability to pay} \times \text{access}}{\text{competition}}$$

Law firms may pay well but are hard to reach and heavily targeted by vendors; joinery workshops may pay less but feel the quoting pain sharply, are easy to reach through trade networks, and are rarely approached. Survey data support looking beyond technology businesses: the UK's ONS reported AI adoption of 58 per cent in information and communication but 13 per cent in construction in 2026, so trades are underserved, though also less familiar with AI.

### Channels

| Channel | Speed | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network and referrals | Fast | Time only | Highest reply and close rates; limited supply |
| Outbound email and LinkedIn | Medium | Time and tools | Scales; must be specific and lawful |
| Content: case studies, posts, talks | Slow | Time | Compounds; builds trust before contact |
| Marketplaces: Upwork, Fiverr | Fast to start | Fees, bidding costs | Upwork charges for Connects used to bid and takes a service fee; competition on price |
| Communities and events | Medium | Time, tickets | Trade associations, local business groups |

Each channel has a **yield**, meetings per hour invested, that you measure. Allocating hours to the highest-yield channels first, up to each channel's useful maximum, maximises meetings:

$$\text{meetings} = \sum_j h_j \times y_j, \qquad \sum_j h_j \le H, \quad 0 \le h_j \le \text{max}_j$$

Greedy allocation by yield is optimal for this simple linear model. Yields change: content starts near zero but grows as people find it.

Platform demand data published by freelance marketplaces show AI-related work growing quickly in 2026, which is real demand but also attracts many sellers, so marketplace profiles need the same specificity as outreach.

### Outreach that earns replies

Effective first messages share a pattern:

- **Short:** under about 120 words.
- **About them first:** the opening line refers to something specific about the prospect, not to you.
- **One proof point:** a specific result for a similar client.
- **One easy ask:** a single question, such as a 15-minute call or permission to send a short example.
- **No generic openers, buzzwords, or link dumps;** at most one link.
- **No unfilled placeholders,** the mark of careless automation.

AI can help research prospects and draft messages, but each message must be checked: generic AI-written outreach is easy to spot and damages your reputation.

### Following up

Many replies come after a follow-up. Two or three polite follow-ups, spaced over a couple of weeks, on business days, are standard; after that, stop. Each follow-up should add something, an example or a relevant observation, rather than "just bumping this".

### Lawful outreach

In the **US**, the CAN-SPAM Act applies to commercial email, including business-to-business: no false or misleading headers or subject lines, identify the message as an advertisement where required, include a valid physical postal address, tell recipients how to opt out, and honour opt-outs promptly, within ten business days. In the **UK**, the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations allow marketing emails to **corporate** subscribers without prior consent if you identify yourself and offer an easy opt-out, but **sole traders and some partnerships** are treated like individuals, needing consent or the soft opt-in; the UK GDPR also applies to the personal data you collect about prospects. Keep a suppression list and honour it.

### Complexity

ICP scoring and linting are linear in fields and message length; niche ranking and channel allocation sort their inputs, $O(n \log n)$; follow-up scheduling is constant per follow-up.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, scoring prospects against the ICP.

```python
import re
from datetime import date, timedelta

ICP = {"industries": {"joinery", "kitchen fitting", "cabinet making"}, "staff": (5, 50),
       "signals": {"slow quotes", "manual pricing", "hiring estimator"}, "min_budget": 2000}

def icp_score(p, icp):
    score, reasons = 0, []
    if p["industry"] in icp["industries"]:
        score += 3
    else:
        reasons.append("industry outside niche")
    lo, hi = icp["staff"]
    if lo <= p["staff"] <= hi:
        score += 2
    else:
        reasons.append("size outside range")
    hits = len(icp["signals"] & set(p["signals"]))
    score += min(hits, 2) * 2
    if hits == 0:
        reasons.append("no pain signals")
    if p["budget"] >= icp["min_budget"]:
        score += 2
    else:
        reasons.append("budget too small")
    return score, reasons

print(icp_score({"industry": "joinery", "staff": 12, "signals": ["slow quotes", "hiring estimator"], "budget": 5000}, ICP))
print(icp_score({"industry": "kitchen fitting", "staff": 60, "signals": ["manual pricing"], "budget": 2000}, ICP))
```

- **Harper Joinery scores the maximum, 11,** with no reasons against.
- **A larger kitchen fitter scores 7:** in the niche with a pain signal, but outside the size range.
- **Reasons explain the score,** so you can decide whether to contact anyway.

Next, ranking niches.

```python
def rank_niches(niches):
    out = [(n["name"], round(n["pain"] * n["ability_to_pay"] * n["access"] / n["competition"], 2)) for n in niches]
    return sorted(out, key=lambda x: -x[1])

NICHES = [{"name": "joinery workshops", "pain": 4, "ability_to_pay": 3, "access": 5, "competition": 2},
          {"name": "law firms", "pain": 4, "ability_to_pay": 5, "access": 2, "competition": 5},
          {"name": "cafes", "pain": 2, "ability_to_pay": 1, "access": 4, "competition": 2}]
print(rank_niches(NICHES))
```

- **Joinery workshops 30, law firms 8, cafes 4.**
- **Law firms pay best but are hard to reach and crowded,** so they rank lower.
- **The ratings are Sam's judgements;** the value is in making them explicit and comparable.

Then, allocating hours across channels.

```python
CHANNELS = [{"name": "network", "meetings_per_hour": 0.5, "max_hours": 2},
            {"name": "outbound email", "meetings_per_hour": 0.15, "max_hours": 6},
            {"name": "content", "meetings_per_hour": 0.05, "max_hours": 4},
            {"name": "marketplace", "meetings_per_hour": 0.1, "max_hours": 5}]

def allocate_hours(channels, hours):
    plan = {c["name"]: 0 for c in channels}
    left = hours
    for c in sorted(channels, key=lambda c: -c["meetings_per_hour"]):
        h = min(c["max_hours"], left)
        plan[c["name"]] = h
        left -= h
        if left <= 0:
            break
    meetings = sum(plan[c["name"]] * c["meetings_per_hour"] for c in channels)
    return plan, round(meetings, 2)

print(allocate_hours(CHANNELS, 10))
print(allocate_hours(CHANNELS, 20))
```

- **Ten hours: 2 on network, 6 on outbound, 2 on the marketplace; about 2.1 meetings a week.**
- **Twenty hours adds content and more marketplace work, but only reaches 2.6 meetings:** each extra hour goes to a lower-yield channel.
- **Yields are illustrative and change.** Content's yield rises over months, so a little time there is an investment.

Then, linting outreach before sending.

```python
def lint_outreach(subject, body):
    issues = []
    words = len(body.split())
    if words > 120:
        issues.append(f"too long ({words} words)")
    if len(subject) > 60:
        issues.append("subject over 60 characters")
    if re.search(r"hope (this|you).{0,20}well", body, re.I):
        issues.append("generic opener")
    first = body.split("\n\n")[1] if "\n\n" in body else body
    if re.match(r"\s*(i am|i'm|my name is)\b", first, re.I):
        issues.append("opens about yourself")
    if body.count("?") != 1:
        issues.append("needs exactly one question as the ask")
    if len(re.findall(r"https?://", body)) > 1:
        issues.append("more than one link")
    if "{" in body or "}" in body:
        issues.append("unfilled placeholder")
    return issues

GOOD = ("Hi Priya,\n\nYour post about quotes taking three hours caught my eye. I built a tool that cut pricing time "
        "by 70 per cent for a joinery shop of your size.\n\nWorth a 15-minute look next week?")
print(lint_outreach("Quick idea for Harper Joinery's quoting", GOOD))
print(lint_outreach("Quick idea", "Hi Priya,\n\nI am an AI consultant. I hope this email finds you well. Call? Demo?"))
```

- **The good message passes:** about them first, one result, one question.
- **The poor one opens about the sender, uses a generic opener, and asks two questions.**
- **Linting catches form, not substance.** Read each message as the recipient would.

Finally, follow-ups on business days.

```python
def follow_ups(sent, gaps=(3, 7, 14)):
    out = []
    d0 = date.fromisoformat(sent)
    for g in gaps:
        d = d0 + timedelta(days=g)
        while d.weekday() >= 5:
            d += timedelta(days=1)
        out.append(d.isoformat())
    return out

print(follow_ups("2026-11-17"), follow_ups("2026-11-20"))
```

- **A Tuesday send gives follow-ups on Friday, the next Tuesday, and the Tuesday after.**
- **A Friday send gives a first follow-up on Monday,** three days later and already a business day; any date that lands on a weekend moves to the following Monday.
- **Stop after three.** Add something new in each.

**Complexity.** Linear or sorting-bound throughout.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**No niche.** Generic offers get generic silence. Choose one.

**Ignoring your network.** Warm introductions convert best. Start there.

**Mass AI-written outreach.** Recipients spot it. Personalise and check.

**Opening about yourself.** Start with them.

**Several asks.** One question is easier to answer.

**Link dumps.** One link at most, often none.

**No follow-up.** Many replies come from follow-ups. Two or three, politely.

**Following up forever.** Stop after three.

**Ignoring the law.** CAN-SPAM, PECR, and GDPR apply. Identify yourself and honour opt-outs.

**Racing to the bottom on marketplaces.** Compete on specificity and proof, not price.

**Only one channel.** Diversify gradually, keeping the best performer.

**Not measuring.** Without reply rates per channel you cannot improve.

def follow_ups(sent, gaps=(3, 7, 14)):
    """Follow-up dates on business days."""
    out = []
    d0 = date.fromisoformat(sent)
    for g in gaps:
        d = d0 + timedelta(days=g)
        while d.weekday() >= 5:
            d += timedelta(days=1)
        out.append(d.isoformat())
    return out
