# AI Hallucinations

In June 2023 a federal judge in New York fined two lawyers USD 5,000. They had filed a legal brief citing six court decisions that did not exist. The cases, complete with plausible names, courts, and quotations, had been produced by ChatGPT. One of the lawyers had even asked the chatbot whether the cases were real, and it had assured him they were. The matter, *Mata v. Avianca*, became the standard cautionary tale about a behaviour everyone who uses language models must understand: **hallucination**, the confident production of statements that are false or unsupported. This chapter explains why it happens, sorts it into types, and gives you the defences that work.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### Plausible is not the same as true

A language model does one thing. It produces a likely continuation of the text so far. For most text, likely and true coincide: the likely continuation of "The capital of France is" is also the correct one. They come apart whenever the model lacks the fact. Asked for the title of an obscure researcher's doctoral thesis, the model has no stored answer. It does have a very good sense of what thesis titles look like. So it produces one, in the right style, with the right vocabulary.

Nothing inside the system marks that output as different from a correct answer. A fabricated citation and a real one are generated by exactly the same process, and they look the same on the page. This is the heart of the problem. Occasional error is unremarkable, since every source makes mistakes. What makes hallucination dangerous is that the errors arrive in the same fluent, confident form as everything else.

The word "hallucination" suggests a malfunction. A more accurate picture is that the model is always doing the same thing, generating plausible text, and we call the output a hallucination when plausibility and truth happen to diverge.

### The exam that rewards guessing

Why does the model not simply say "I don't know"? Think about a multiple-choice exam where a correct answer earns one mark and both a wrong answer and a blank earn zero. What should a student do with a question they cannot answer? Guess, every time. A guess might score. A blank never does. A student who always guesses will outscore an equally knowledgeable student who honestly leaves blanks.

Language models are trained and ranked largely on exams of exactly this kind. Benchmarks report accuracy: the fraction of answers that are right. Saying "I don't know" counts the same as being wrong. A model that bluffs therefore scores higher on the leaderboard than a model that admits uncertainty, and the systems that reach the public are the ones that scored well. This argument was set out in a September 2025 paper by researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech, and it reframes the problem: in their account, hallucination is partly a predictable response to how models are graded.

### The detective and the witness

Keep two roles in mind for the rest of the chapter. A model answering from its parameters alone is a **witness** recalling events from memory: often right, sometimes wrong, and unable to tell you which. A model given the relevant documents and asked to answer from them is a **detective** working from evidence on the table. The detective can still misread the evidence, but the error rate is far lower and, crucially, the work can be checked. Most practical defences against hallucination consist of turning the witness into a detective and then checking the detective's work.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### Why hallucinations happen: three causes

**Cause 1: the training objective.** Pre-training minimises the cross-entropy loss, which rewards assigning high probability to the text that actually occurred. Nothing in that objective distinguishes a true sentence from a false sentence with the same statistical shape. The 2025 paper makes the consequence precise for facts that follow no pattern. A person's birthday cannot be deduced. It can only be memorised. If a fact appears just once in the training data, the model has had essentially one chance to learn it. The authors show that if, say, 20 percent of the birthday facts in the training data appear exactly once, a base model should be expected to get at least about 20 percent of such facts wrong. Errors on rare, arbitrary facts are a statistical consequence of the method, even with perfectly clean training data.

**Cause 2: evaluation rewards guessing.** This is the exam argument, and it can be made exact. Let $p$ be the probability that the model's best guess is correct. Let a correct answer score $+1$, an abstention score $0$, and a wrong answer score $-w$, where $w \ge 0$ is the penalty. The expected score from guessing is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{guess}] = p \cdot 1 + (1 - p)\cdot(-w) = p - (1-p)\,w
$$

Guessing beats abstaining when this is positive, which rearranges to:

$$
p > \frac{w}{1 + w}
$$

Under plain accuracy scoring, $w = 0$, and the condition becomes $p > 0$. Any guess with any chance of being right is worth making. That is the incentive to bluff.

Now suppose we want the model to answer only when it is at least $t$ confident. Set the penalty to $w = \dfrac{t}{1-t}$. Substituting:

$$
\frac{w}{1+w} = \frac{t/(1-t)}{1 + t/(1-t)} = \frac{t/(1-t)}{1/(1-t)} = t
$$

so guessing pays only when $p > t$. With $t = 0.75$ the penalty is 3: a wrong answer costs three marks. Under that rule a model that is only 60 percent sure does better by saying "I don't know". The grading scheme decides the behaviour.

| Grading scheme | Penalty $w$ | Guess when confidence exceeds |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy only | 0 | 0% (always guess) |
| Wrong costs one mark | 1 | 50% |
| Wrong costs three marks | 3 | 75% |
| Wrong costs nine marks | 9 | 90% |

**Cause 3: the mechanism for declining misfires.** Interpretability research at Anthropic, which examines the internal activity of a model, found that in the model they studied, declining to answer is the default behaviour: there is internal circuitry that leads to "I can't answer that" unless something suppresses it. A separate feature that responds to *known entities* provides the suppression. When the model recognises a name well, that feature switches off the refusal and the model answers. A hallucination can occur when the feature fires for a name that is only *familiar*, recognised but with no facts attached. The refusal is suppressed, and with nothing real to say, the model produces something plausible. This is a finding about one model and the general picture should be held loosely, but it matches experience: models hallucinate most about things they half know.

```text
                    question about an entity
                             |
                 +-----------+-----------+
                 |                       |
        entity well known          entity unfamiliar
                 |                       |
     refusal suppressed,        default refusal stays on
     facts available                     |
                 |               "I don't know"   (correct behaviour)
          correct answer
                 
                 entity only FAMILIAR (name recognised, facts missing)
                             |
                 refusal suppressed, NO facts available
                             |
                  fluent invented answer   <-- hallucination
```

### A taxonomy

Not all hallucinations are the same, and the defences differ by type.

| Type | What goes wrong | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Factual error | A statement contradicts the real world | Giving the wrong year for a historical event |
| Fabrication | An entity or source is invented | A court case, paper, URL, or quotation that does not exist |
| Faithfulness error | The output contradicts or goes beyond a document the model was given | A summary that includes a claim absent from the source |
| Instruction error | The output ignores or contradicts the user's instruction | Asked for three items, it gives five; told to use only the provided text, it adds outside claims |

The distinction between the first two and the third is the most useful. **Factuality** concerns agreement with the world. **Faithfulness** concerns agreement with the supplied source. A summary can be factually true and still unfaithful, if it adds true information that was not in the document. In professional work, faithfulness is usually what you need and it is also the easier one to check, because the source is right there.

### Mitigations

**1. Grounding.** Put the relevant source text into the context and instruct the model to answer from it. Systems that fetch documents automatically and insert them are called **retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**, the subject of a later module. Grounding converts the witness into a detective. It reduces hallucination substantially and does not eliminate it: the model can still misread, over-generalise from, or ignore the supplied text.

**2. Quote first, then answer.** For long documents, ask the model to extract the exact passages relevant to the question before answering, and to base its answer only on those quotes. This is one of the techniques recommended in Anthropic's documentation, and it works because the intermediate quotes are easy to verify against the source.

**3. Citations that are checked.** Require each claim to carry a pointer to the passage that supports it, and instruct the model to withdraw any claim for which it cannot find support. Then verify that the cited passage exists and says what is claimed. A citation that nobody checks is decoration. The lawyers in *Mata v. Avianca* had citations.

**4. Permission to abstain.** State explicitly that "I don't know" or "the document does not say" is an acceptable answer. This shifts the model toward the behaviour that accuracy-only training discouraged.

**5. Self-consistency.** Ask the same question several times with sampling turned on and compare the answers. The idea, used for detection in the 2023 SelfCheckGPT paper, is that knowledge is stable and invention is not: a fact the model has learned tends to reappear in every sample, while a fabricated detail tends to differ from sample to sample. If $n$ samples are drawn and the most common answer appears $m$ times, the **agreement** is:

$$
\text{agreement} = \frac{m}{n}
$$

High agreement suggests the answer is stable. Low agreement is a warning. To see why voting helps, suppose each sample is independently correct with probability $q = 0.7$ and wrong answers are scattered. With five samples, the correct answer wins a majority with probability

$$
\sum_{k=3}^{5} \binom{5}{k}\, 0.7^{k}\, 0.3^{5-k} = 0.309 + 0.360 + 0.168 \approx 0.837
$$

which is better than the 0.7 of a single sample. A refinement called semantic entropy groups answers by meaning before comparing them, so that "Paris" and "the city of Paris" count as agreeing.

**6. Independent verification.** Check claims against an authoritative source: a database, a search, a calculation, or a person. For anything with consequences this is the only complete defence.

**Costs.** None of this is free. Self-consistency with $n$ samples costs roughly $n$ times as much and takes longer. Grounding lengthens prompts. Verification costs human time. As the previous module said, the amount of checking should match the cost of being wrong.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

The following program implements three of the defences with nothing but the standard library. The "model" is a list of pre-written sample answers, so the logic can be studied without an API.

```python
import re
from collections import Counter

def majority_answer(samples):
    """Return (most common answer, agreement). Answers are compared case-insensitively."""
    if not samples:
        return None, 0.0
    counts = Counter(s.strip().lower() for s in samples)
    best = min(counts, key=lambda answer: (-counts[answer], answer))
    return best, counts[best] / len(samples)

def answer_or_abstain(samples, threshold=0.6):
    """Answer only when the samples agree strongly enough."""
    answer, agreement = majority_answer(samples)
    return answer if agreement >= threshold else "I don't know"

STOPWORDS = {"that", "this", "with", "from", "were", "have", "been", "which", "their", "about"}

def content_words(text):
    """Lower-case words of 4+ characters, minus a few common ones."""
    return {w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower()) if len(w) >= 4 and w not in STOPWORDS}

def unsupported_sentences(answer, source, min_overlap=0.5):
    """Flag answer sentences whose content words are mostly absent from the source."""
    source_words = content_words(source)
    flagged = []
    for sentence in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", answer.strip()):
        words = content_words(sentence)
        if not words:
            continue
        overlap = len(words & source_words) / len(words)
        if overlap < min_overlap:
            flagged.append(sentence)
    return flagged

known = ["Paris", "paris", "Paris ", "Paris", "Lyon"]                    # a fact the model knows
invented = ["Dr. A. Rao", "Prof. Lin", "J. Smith", "Dr. A. Rao", "M. Okafor"]   # a fact it does not
print(majority_answer(known), "->", answer_or_abstain(known))
print(majority_answer(invented), "->", answer_or_abstain(invented))

source = "The Eiffel Tower is located in Paris. It was completed in 1889 and stands 330 metres tall."
answer = "The Eiffel Tower stands in Paris. It was completed in 1889. It was designed by Leonardo da Vinci."
print(unsupported_sentences(answer, source))
```

What each part is doing:

- **`majority_answer` normalises before counting.** "Paris", "paris", and "Paris " are the same answer. Without `strip().lower()` they would be counted as three, and agreement would be understated. Real systems go further and compare meanings.
- **The tie-break is deterministic.** `min` over `(-count, answer)` picks the most frequent answer and, among equals, the alphabetically first. Two runs on the same samples always give the same result.
- **`answer_or_abstain` turns agreement into a decision.** The first set of samples agrees four times in five, 0.8, and is answered. The second has a top agreement of two in five, 0.4, and the function abstains. This is the exam-scoring idea implemented: the threshold is the confidence level below which the system is told to say "I don't know".
- **`content_words` ignores short and common words.** Words such as "the", "was", and "in" appear in every text and would make every sentence look supported.
- **`unsupported_sentences` checks faithfulness sentence by sentence.** The first two sentences of the answer are built from words in the source. The third introduces "designed", "Leonardo", and "Vinci", none of which appear, so it is flagged. It is the fabricated one.
- **The sentence splitter uses a lookbehind.** `(?<=[.!?])\s+` splits on whitespace that follows sentence-ending punctuation while keeping the punctuation attached to the sentence.

This grounding check is deliberately crude, and its weaknesses are instructive. Change the answer's second sentence to "It was completed in 1989." Half its content words ("completed") are still in the source, so at the default threshold it passes, although the year is wrong. Word overlap cannot tell that a number changed, that a "not" was dropped, or that two true facts were combined into a false one. It is a cheap first filter that catches wholesale invention. Production systems use a second model to judge whether each claim is entailed by the source, and still keep a human in the loop for high-stakes output.

The scoring argument from Section 2 fits in a few lines:

```python
def expected_score(p, wrong_penalty):
    return p - (1 - p) * wrong_penalty

for penalty in (0, 1, 3):
    row = ", ".join(f"p={p}: {expected_score(p, penalty):+.2f}" for p in (0.2, 0.6, 0.9))
    print(f"wrong answer costs {penalty}: {row}")
```

With a penalty of zero every value is positive, so guessing always pays. With a penalty of three, only the 0.9-confidence guess is worth making. Nothing about the model changed between the rows. Only the grading did.

**Complexity.** Voting over $n$ samples is linear in $n$. The grounding check is linear in the length of the answer plus the source, because set membership tests take constant time. The real cost is the $n$ model calls behind the samples.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Asking the model "are you sure?" is not verification.** The lawyer in *Mata v. Avianca* asked ChatGPT whether a case was real and was told that it was. A model asked to confirm its own output generates a plausible reply to that question, and "yes" is plausible. Verification means checking against something outside the model.

**Hallucinations cluster in predictable places.** Expect them in citations, URLs, quotations, statistics, dates, names of minor figures, version numbers, API details, and anything recent or obscure. Experienced users check exactly these and skim the rest.

**Retrieval reduces hallucination and relocates the problem.** With documents in the context, errors become faithfulness errors: the model cites a real source for something the source does not say. Checking that a citation exists is not enough. Check that it supports the claim.

**A confident tone is not calibration.** Models express certainty by default. Phrases such as "definitely" or "it is well established that" carry no information about correctness.

**Self-consistency catches random errors, not systematic ones.** If the model has learned something false, it will say the same false thing every time, with perfect agreement. Voting helps when the model is guessing. It does nothing when the model is consistently wrong.

**Lower temperature does not supply missing knowledge.** It makes the model more repeatable. If the fact is absent, a temperature of zero produces the same invention every time, which can make it look more trustworthy than it is.

**A higher benchmark score may mean more guessing.** On accuracy-only leaderboards, a model that abstains appropriately is penalised relative to one that bluffs. When reliability matters, look for evaluations that report error rates and abstention rates separately.

**"Do not hallucinate" in a prompt does very little.** The model does not know which of its statements are invented. Instructions that change the task work much better: "answer only from the text below", "quote the supporting passage", "say 'not stated' if the text does not cover it".

**Newer models hallucinate less, not never.** Both major vendors say plainly that their current models still produce false statements. Reasoning models and search tools have cut the rate on many tasks. The appropriate response to a lower rate is not to stop checking. A rare error in a trusted system is more dangerous than a frequent error in a distrusted one.

**Responsibility does not transfer.** The court's reasoning in *Mata v. Avianca* was not that using AI was wrong. It was that the lawyers signed a filing without checking it. In professional work the person who submits the output owns it.

## 5. Active Recall Check & Synthesis Summary

**Summary.** A hallucination is a fluent, confident statement that is false or unsupported, produced by the same process as every correct statement and indistinguishable from one by its form. It arises because training rewards plausible text, because arbitrary facts seen rarely cannot be reliably learned, because accuracy-only evaluation makes guessing the winning strategy, and because the internal mechanism for declining can be suppressed by mere familiarity. Hallucinations divide into factual errors, fabrications, faithfulness errors, and instruction errors. The defences are grounding the model in source text, extracting quotes before answering, requiring and checking citations, explicitly permitting abstention, comparing several sampled answers for agreement, and independent verification. None removes the problem entirely, so the level of checking must match the stakes, and the person who uses the output remains responsible for it.

**Question 1.** Using the expected-score formula, show why a model graded on accuracy alone should always guess, and compute the minimum confidence at which guessing is worthwhile when a wrong answer costs four marks.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

The expected score of guessing is p minus (1 minus p) times w, where p is the chance the guess is right and w is the penalty for a wrong answer. Abstaining scores zero. With accuracy-only grading w is 0, so the expected score of guessing is simply p, which is positive whenever there is any chance of being right. Guessing therefore never does worse than abstaining and usually does better. With w equal to 4, guessing is worthwhile only when p is greater than w divided by (1 plus w), which is 4 divided by 5, or 0.8. Below 80 percent confidence the model scores better by saying "I don't know". The penalty, not the model, determines when it should answer.

</details>

**Question 2.** A summary of a company report contains the sentence "Revenue grew 12 percent, driven by strong demand in Asia." The report says revenue grew 12 percent and never mentions Asia. Strong Asian demand was in fact the cause, as reported elsewhere. Classify the error and explain why it matters.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

It is a faithfulness error. The claim about Asia is true in the world, so it is not a factual error, but it is not supported by the source the model was asked to summarise. It matters because the reader of a summary assumes every statement comes from the document. Here the model added information from its own parameters, and it happened to be right. The same behaviour will, on another occasion, add something wrong, and nobody will be able to tell the difference by looking. In professional settings a summary must be traceable to its source, so unsupported additions are defects even when they are true.

</details>

**Question 3.** You ask a model the same question five times and receive the identical answer each time. Does this establish that the answer is correct? Explain what self-consistency can and cannot detect.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

No. Agreement shows that the answer is stable, which rules out the kind of hallucination where the model is improvising and produces a different invention each time. It does not rule out a systematic error: if the model has learned something false, or the question contains a misleading premise that pushes every sample the same way, all five answers will agree and all five will be wrong. Self-consistency detects uncertainty that shows up as variation. It cannot detect confident, consistent error. For that you need verification against an external source, such as a supplied document, a database, or a person with the relevant expertise.

</details>

import re
from collections import Counter

STOPWORDS = {"that", "this", "with", "from", "were", "have", "been", "which", "their", "about"}


def content_words(text):
    """Lower-case words of 4 or more characters, minus a few very common ones."""
    return {w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower()) if len(w) >= 4 and w not in STOPWORDS}


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 1
def expected_score(p, wrong_penalty):
    """With wrong_penalty = 0 (accuracy-only grading) this is just p: guessing always pays."""
    return p - (1 - p) * wrong_penalty


def should_answer(p, wrong_penalty):
    """Answer only when guessing beats the 0 marks earned by abstaining.

    Equivalent to p > wrong_penalty / (1 + wrong_penalty).
    """
    return expected_score(p, wrong_penalty) > 0


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 2
def majority_answer(samples):
    """Self-consistency vote.

    Knowledge is stable across samples; invention varies. Normalising first means
    "Paris", "paris" and "Paris " are counted as one answer.
    """
    if not samples:
        return None, 0.0
    counts = Counter(sample.strip().lower() for sample in samples)

def answer_or_abstain(samples, threshold=0.6):
    """Turn agreement into a decision: below the threshold, say so honestly."""
    answer, agreement = majority_answer(samples)
    if answer is None or agreement < threshold:
        return "I don't know"
    return answer

def unsupported_sentences(answer, source, min_overlap=0.5):
    """A cheap faithfulness check.

    It catches wholesale invention. It does NOT catch a changed number, a dropped
    "not", or two true facts merged into a false one. Treat it as a first filter.
    """
    source_words = content_words(source)
    flagged = []
    for sentence in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", answer.strip()):
        words = content_words(sentence)
        if not words:
            continue                               # nothing checkable in this sentence
        overlap = len(words & source_words) / len(words)
        if overlap < min_overlap:
            flagged.append(sentence)
    return flagged
