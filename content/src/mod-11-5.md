# Building a Portfolio

A prospect deciding whether to hire you is asking one question: **can this person solve my problem?** A portfolio answers it with evidence: problems you solved, results you measured, and demos they can see working. For AI work this matters more than usual, because many prospects have seen impressive demos that never became useful tools; what persuades them is proof of outcomes in situations like theirs. This module covers choosing portfolio projects that demonstrate outcomes, writing case studies, and presenting demos, proof, and a modest social presence, with honesty as a constraint throughout.

The joinery figures are illustrative. Rules about testimonials are from the US Federal Trade Commission, cited at the end, at the time of writing (November 2026).

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### The joiner's photo album

A joiner's best sales tool is a photo album of finished work: before and after pictures of real kitchens, with a note from the customer. Nobody hires a joiner because of the brand of their saw. Each page shows a **problem**, a **result**, and **proof** that it was real. A portfolio of AI work is the same album: not "I used LangGraph and Qdrant", but "quotes took three hours; now they take thirty minutes; here is the owner saying so".

```text
 PROJECT                          PROOF STRENGTH                          WHERE IT GOES
 client project with result  ->  outcome + real user + demo + write-up   case study page, outreach, proposals
 practice project on real data   outcome measured on realistic data       portfolio, posts ("build log")
 tutorial clone                  shows effort only                        not in the portfolio

 case study:  client | problem | approach | result (before -> after, per cent) | quote (real, with permission)
 demo:        under 3 minutes, no login, anonymised data, working links
 presence:    one post a week from your own projects: build log, result, lesson
```

### Outcomes beat technology

The technology matters to you; the outcome matters to the client. Every portfolio piece should lead with a **measured improvement** in terms the client uses: minutes, errors, enquiries, money. Technical details belong further down, for the technical reader who wants them.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### Portfolio projects that demonstrate outcomes

The strongest pieces have five properties, roughly in order of weight:

1. **A measured outcome:** a before and after number.
2. **A real user:** a client or at least a real business process, not a toy.
3. **A demo:** a short video or a live example a prospect can try.
4. **A written case study:** the story in a page.
5. **Viewable work:** code, a workflow export, or screenshots for technical readers.

Without clients yet, build **practice projects on realistic problems** in your niche, ideally with a friendly business, a volunteer organisation, or public data, and measure them honestly. Label them as practice projects; never present them as client work.

### Stating improvements

A result is clearest as a before, an after, and a percentage change. For a quantity that should go down, such as time or errors,

$$\text{improvement} = \frac{\text{before} - \text{after}}{\text{before}} \times 100\%$$

and for one that should go up, such as enquiries answered, the change relative to the before value. "Cut quoting time from 180 to 30 minutes, 83 per cent" is concrete; "dramatically faster quoting" is not. Percentages above 100 for increases are legitimate but invite scepticism, so give both numbers.

### Writing case studies

A case study has a fixed structure that reads in under a minute:

- **Headline:** client and result.
- **Problem:** in the client's words, with the cost if known, Module 11.3.
- **Approach:** what you did, briefly, and why.
- **Result:** the measured improvement.
- **Quote:** from the client, real and with permission.

Write it with the client's approval, especially before naming them. If the client prefers anonymity, describe them generically, "a 12-person joinery workshop", and keep the numbers.

### Honest proof

Testimonials must be real. The US Federal Trade Commission's rule on reviews and testimonials, finalised in August 2024, bans fake or AI-generated testimonials and reviews, among other practices, and enables civil penalties; misleading testimonials are also unlawful under consumer protection law in the UK and elsewhere. Do not invent clients, inflate numbers, or present practice projects as paid work. Honest, modest proof is more persuasive to experienced buyers anyway.

### Demos

A demo should work for a busy prospect in a few minutes: a **video under three minutes**, or a live example that needs **no login**, uses **anonymised data**, and has **no broken links**. Client data must never appear in a public demo; replace names, prices, and anything identifying, in line with data minimisation under the GDPR.

### Social presence

A small, steady presence on the one platform your niche uses, often LinkedIn for business owners, compounds over time, Module 11.4. A simple rhythm is one post a week, rotating through your projects and through three kinds of post: a **build log** showing the work, a **result** showing the outcome, and a **lesson** about something that went wrong or surprised you. Lessons often earn the most trust.

### Choosing which projects to build first

Before clients arrive, choose practice projects deliberately rather than building whatever is fashionable. Three tests help. **Niche fit:** does the project solve a problem your ideal client profile recognises, Module 11.4? A quote assistant speaks to joinery workshops; a generic chatbot speaks to no one. **Measurability:** can you produce an honest before and after number, from timestamps, a sample of real documents, or a timed test? A project with no measurable outcome cannot become a strong case study however clever it is. **Demonstrability:** can a prospect see it working in under three minutes without your help? If the answer to any test is no, change the project before building it.

A useful order is: first, a small project for a real business, even unpaid or at a reduced rate, in exchange for permission to publish the result and a genuine quote; second, a practice project on realistic, anonymised data in your niche; third, an improvement to an earlier project that adds a new result. Three pieces chosen this way usually beat ten assorted experiments.

### Keeping the portfolio current

A portfolio decays: links break, screenshots show old interfaces, and results from two years ago look stale next to newer work. Review it monthly, check links, replace the weakest piece when a stronger one arrives, and update case studies when clients report longer-term results, such as savings over a year rather than a month. Old work need not be deleted, but the first three pieces a prospect sees should be the strongest and most relevant to them, which may mean a different order for different niches.

### Complexity

Scoring projects and checking demos are linear in their fields; generating a case study is linear in its text; a content calendar is linear in weeks.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, scoring projects as proof.

```python
import re

def project_score(p):
    checks = [("outcome", "has a measured outcome", 3), ("real_user", "used by a real client or user", 2),
              ("demo", "has a demo or video", 2), ("writeup", "has a written case study", 2),
              ("code", "code or workflow is viewable", 1)]
    score, gaps = 0, []
    for key, label, pts in checks:
        if p.get(key):
            score += pts
        else:
            gaps.append(label)
    return score, gaps

print(project_score({"outcome": True, "real_user": True, "demo": True, "writeup": False, "code": True}))
print(project_score({"code": True}))
```

- **The client project scores 8 of 10;** the missing piece is the written case study.
- **A code-only project scores 1.** A GitHub link alone proves little to a business owner.
- **Gaps are a to-do list.** Fixing the case study is cheaper than starting a new project.

Next, stating improvements.

```python
def improvement(before, after, unit, lower_is_better=True):
    change = (before - after) / before if lower_is_better else (after - before) / before
    verb = "cut" if lower_is_better else "raised"
    return f"{verb} {unit} from {before:g} to {after:g} ({round(change * 100)} per cent)"

print(improvement(180, 30, "quoting time in minutes"))
print(improvement(12, 30, "enquiries answered same day", lower_is_better=False))
```

- **"Cut quoting time in minutes from 180 to 30 (83 per cent)."**
- **Increases are stated relative to the starting value:** 12 to 30 is a 150 per cent rise. Give both numbers so readers can judge.
- **The unit is explicit,** so the number cannot be misread.

Then, generating a case study.

```python
REQUIRED = ["client", "problem", "approach", "before", "after", "unit"]

def case_study(f):
    missing = [k for k in REQUIRED if k not in f or f[k] in ("", None)]
    if missing:
        return None, missing
    result = improvement(f["before"], f["after"], f["unit"], f.get("lower_is_better", True))
    lines = [f"## {f['client']}: {result}", f"**Problem.** {f['problem']}", f"**Approach.** {f['approach']}",
             f"**Result.** {result[0].upper() + result[1:]}."]
    if f.get("quote"):
        lines.append(f"> {f['quote']}")
    return "\n\n".join(lines), []

HARPER = {"client": "Harper Joinery", "problem": "Pricing materials took 20 minutes per quote, with frequent errors.",
          "approach": "A pricing assistant that reads the supplier price list and drafts line items for review.",
          "before": 20, "after": 6, "unit": "pricing time per quote in minutes",
          "quote": "We quote the same day now. - Priya, owner"}
text, missing = case_study(HARPER)
print(text)
print(case_study({"client": "Example"})[1])
```

- **The headline leads with the client and the result.**
- **A case study missing the problem, approach, or numbers is refused,** with a list of what is missing.
- **The quote is optional and must be real;** Priya approved it.

Then, checking demos.

```python
def demo_issues(d):
    issues = []
    if d["minutes"] > 3:
        issues.append("video over 3 minutes")
    if d.get("requires_login"):
        issues.append("demo requires a login")
    if not d.get("anonymised"):
        issues.append("client data not anonymised")
    bad = [u for u, s in d.get("links", {}).items() if s >= 400]
    if bad:
        issues.append("broken links: " + ", ".join(sorted(bad)))
    return issues

print(demo_issues({"minutes": 5, "requires_login": True, "anonymised": False,
                   "links": {"https://demo.example": 200, "https://old.example": 404}}))
print(demo_issues({"minutes": 2.5, "anonymised": True, "links": {"https://demo.example": 200}}))
```

- **The first demo fails four checks,** including showing client data.
- **The second passes.** Link statuses would come from a periodic check in practice.
- **Anonymisation is not optional:** a public demo with a client's prices breaches their trust and possibly data protection law.

Finally, a content calendar.

```python
def calendar(projects, weeks, kinds=("build log", "result", "lesson")):
    plan = []
    for i in range(weeks):
        p = projects[i % len(projects)]
        k = kinds[(i // len(projects)) % len(kinds)]
        plan.append((i + 1, p, k))
    return plan

for row in calendar(["quote assistant", "chaser", "price checker"], 7):
    print(row)
```

- **Projects rotate weekly; the kind of post changes each cycle.**
- **Seven weeks of posts from three projects,** without inventing anything new.
- **One post a week is sustainable;** consistency matters more than volume.

**Complexity.** Linear throughout.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**A list of GitHub links.** Business owners do not read code. Lead with outcomes.

**Leading with the tech stack.** Put tools at the end.

**Vague results.** "Faster" convinces no one. Give numbers.

**Fake or AI-generated testimonials.** Banned under the FTC rule and misleading anywhere.

**Practice projects passed off as client work.** Label them honestly.

**Public demos with client data.** Anonymise everything.

**Long demo videos.** Under three minutes, with the result first.

**Broken links.** Check them regularly.

**Too many pieces.** Three strong ones beat ten weak ones.

**Never updating.** Add new results as they arrive.

**Posting everywhere.** Pick the one platform your niche uses.

**No permission.** Ask clients before naming them or quoting them.
