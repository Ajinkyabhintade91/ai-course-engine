# Discovery Call Framework

Module 11.9 explained why discovery calls matter and how to run them. This bonus module packages that into a **framework** you can reuse: an agenda that scales to the time available, a **question bank** organised by stage with must-ask questions marked, a **post-call summary template** that shows at a glance what is still unknown, a follow-up email, and a simple score for each call so you improve over time. Keep it open during calls and fill the summary immediately afterwards.

Examples continue the Harper Joinery call; timings and thresholds are illustrative starting points.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### The joiner's survey sheet

A joiner surveying a room for fitted furniture carries a printed survey sheet: room dimensions, wall construction, sockets and pipes, the customer's wishes, budget, deadline, and access. The sheet makes sure nothing is forgotten, however chatty the customer or rushed the visit, and it means the quote back at the workshop is written from facts, not memory. The discovery framework is that survey sheet for conversations.

```text
 AGENDA (30 min)                         QUESTION BANK                        AFTER THE CALL
  3 rapport and purpose                  process   * walk me through          SUMMARY: problem in their words |
  8 current process                      impact    * hours a week               numbers | decision makers | budget |
  6 impact and cost                                * what goes wrong            timing | next step | open questions
  5 desired outcome                      outcome   * what's different in 3 mo  FOLLOW-UP EMAIL: their words +
  5 budget, decision, timing             qualify   * who decides, * budget       next step + date
  3 next steps                           (* = must ask)                       SCORE: stages, talk ratio, dated
 scale to 20 or 45 minutes, keeping proportions and the total exact            next step, quotes captured
```

### Consistency is the point

Every call is different, but the information a proposal needs is always the same: the problem in the client's words, numbers, who decides, budget, timing, and an agreed next step. A framework makes sure every call collects them, and the summary shows immediately which are missing.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### The agenda and its timing

The 30-minute agenda from Module 11.9 allocates 3, 8, 6, 5, 5, and 3 minutes to rapport and purpose, current process, impact and cost, desired outcome, budget, decision, and timing, and next steps. Calls are not always 30 minutes; scaling keeps the proportions while making the minutes whole and the total exact. The **largest remainder method**, used for allocating seats in some elections, does this: give each stage the whole-number part of its scaled time, then hand the leftover minutes to the stages with the largest remainders, breaking ties in agenda order:

$$m_i = \left\lfloor \frac{w_i L}{W} \right\rfloor + [\,i \text{ among the top } r \text{ remainders}\,], \qquad r = L - \sum_i \left\lfloor \frac{w_i L}{W} \right\rfloor$$

for base weights $w_i$ summing to $W$ and a call of $L$ minutes. A 45-minute call gets 5, 12, 9, 8, 7, and 4 minutes; a 20-minute call gets 2, 6, 4, 3, 3, and 2.

### Opening well

The first minutes set the tone: thank the client, confirm the time available, state the purpose, "to understand how quoting works today and whether I can help, and if so agree a next step", and ask permission to ask questions and take notes. This makes the call a structured conversation rather than a pitch.

### The question bank

Questions are grouped by stage, with **must-ask** questions marked. Following Rob Fitzpatrick's advice in *The Mom Test*, they ask about past behaviour and specifics rather than opinions:

| Stage | Question | Must ask |
|---|---|---|
| Process | Walk me through how this happens today. | yes |
| Process | Who is involved at each step? | no |
| Impact | How many hours a week does it take? | yes |
| Impact | What happens when it goes wrong? | yes |
| Impact | How often does that happen? | no |
| Outcome | What would be different in three months if this were fixed? | yes |
| Qualify | Who else is involved in deciding? | yes |
| Qualify | Have you set a budget for this? | yes |
| Qualify | When do you need it working? | no |

In a short call, ask the must-asks first and the others if time allows. The qualification questions follow BANT, budget, authority, need, and timing; MEDDIC and CHAMP are alternatives for larger organisations, Module 11.9.

### The post-call summary

Immediately after the call, fill seven sections: **problem in their words**, **numbers**, **decision makers**, **budget**, **timing**, **next step**, and **open questions**. Any empty section is an explicit gap. A summary with gaps is not a failure; a proposal written over gaps is. Gaps go into the follow-up email or the next call.

### The follow-up email

Send a short email the same day: thank the client, restate the problem in their own words, which shows you listened, and confirm the next step with a date. It doubles as a written record of what was agreed.

### Scoring the call

A simple score helps you improve: 2 points for each core stage covered, process, impact, outcome, and qualification; 2 for a talk ratio of 0.4 or less, Module 11.9; 2 for a dated next step; and 1 for capturing at least two exact quotes. A maximum of 13; 10 or more is good, 6 to 9 fair, below 6 means the call should be redone or followed up properly.

### Listening for evidence, not opinions

The question bank asks about what has happened, not what might happen, because people are generous with opinions and stingy with facts. "Would a faster quoting tool help?" invites a polite yes that predicts nothing. "When did you last lose a job because a quote was late, and what was it worth?" produces a date, an amount, and a story Sam can quote back in the proposal. When a client answers with a generality, such as "it is always a pain", the follow-up is "tell me about the last time": the specific case reveals the steps, the people, and the cost. Three signals separate a real problem from a mild annoyance: the client has already tried to fix it, perhaps with a spreadsheet or a temporary hire; the problem has a number attached that they know without looking it up; and they bring up the next step before Sam does. A call without any of the three usually ends in a polite proposal request and no decision.

### Handling a call that goes off the agenda

Real calls rarely follow the timetable. If the client spends ten minutes on one painful story, that is usually good: it is the impact stage arriving early, so Sam notes the numbers and quotes and shortens the stage later. If the client jumps straight to price, Sam gives an honest range, "similar projects run from about GBP 750 to GBP 6,000 depending on scope", and asks what they would need to see to justify the upper end, which returns the conversation to outcomes. If the call is about to end with qualification untouched, Sam says so plainly, "before we finish, two quick questions so I do not waste your time with the wrong proposal", and asks the two must-ask qualification questions. The agenda is a budget of minutes, not a script; the summary sections are what must be filled.

### Preparing the night before

Five minutes of preparation changes the first ten minutes of the call. Sam reads the client's website and any reviews, notes one specific observation to open with, such as a recent project photo, checks which of the ideal customer profile criteria from Module 11.4 are already known, and writes down the one hypothesis Sam most wants to test, for example that quoting is slow because materials are priced by hand. Preparation lets the rapport stage be genuine rather than generic, and it means the questions can skip what is already public.

### Complexity

Scaling the agenda sorts the stages by remainder, $O(s \log s)$; picking questions, building the summary, and scoring are linear in their inputs.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, scaling the agenda.

```python
from datetime import date

BASE = [("rapport and purpose", 3), ("current process", 8), ("impact and cost", 6),
        ("desired outcome", 5), ("budget, decision, timing", 5), ("next steps", 3)]

def scale_agenda(length, base=BASE):
    total = sum(m for _, m in base)
    mins = [m * length // total for _, m in base]
    rems = [m * length % total for _, m in base]
    left = length - sum(mins)
    for i in sorted(range(len(base)), key=lambda i: (-rems[i], i))[:left]:
        mins[i] += 1
    return [(n, m) for (n, _), m in zip(base, mins)]

print(scale_agenda(45))
print(scale_agenda(20))
```

- **45 minutes: 5, 12, 9, 8, 7, 4,** exactly 45 in total.
- **20 minutes: 2, 6, 4, 3, 3, 2,** with the spare minute going to the current process.
- **Integer arithmetic for the remainders** avoids floating-point ties deciding the result.

Next, picking questions.

```python
BANK = [("process", "Walk me through how this happens today.", True), ("process", "Who is involved at each step?", False),
        ("impact", "How many hours a week does it take?", True), ("impact", "What happens when it goes wrong?", True),
        ("impact", "How often does that happen?", False),
        ("outcome", "What would be different in three months if this were fixed?", True),
        ("qualify", "Who else is involved in deciding?", True), ("qualify", "Have you set a budget for this?", True),
        ("qualify", "When do you need it working?", False)]

def pick(bank, stages, per_stage):
    out = []
    for s in stages:
        qs = sorted([b for b in bank if b[0] == s], key=lambda b: not b[2])
        out += [q for _, q, _ in qs[:per_stage]]
    return out

for q in pick(BANK, ["process", "impact", "outcome", "qualify"], 2):
    print(q)
```

- **Seven questions for a 30-minute call,** two per stage where available, must-asks first.
- **The sort is stable,** so questions keep their bank order within must-asks and optional ones.

Then, the post-call summary.

```python
SECTIONS = ["problem in their words", "numbers", "decision makers", "budget", "timing", "next step", "open questions"]

def summary(notes):
    missing = [s for s in SECTIONS if not notes.get(s)]
    lines = [f"{s.upper()}: {notes[s]}" for s in SECTIONS if notes.get(s)]
    return lines, missing

NOTES = {"problem in their words": "Quotes take three hours", "numbers": "10 hours a week pricing materials",
         "decision makers": "Priya", "budget": "GBP 3,000", "timing": "within a month", "next step": "proposal sent"}
lines, missing = summary(NOTES)
print(lines[0], missing)
```

- **Six of seven sections filled; open questions is empty,** which is fine when nothing is outstanding but should be a conscious choice.

Then, the follow-up email.

```python
def follow_up(name, notes, next_date):
    d = date.fromisoformat(next_date).strftime("%A %d %B").replace(" 0", " ")
    return f"Hi {name}, thanks for today. You said: \"{notes['problem in their words']}\". Next: {notes['next step']} by {d}."

print(follow_up("Priya", NOTES, "2026-11-17"))
```

- **Restates Priya's words and the next step with a date:** "by Tuesday 17 November".
- **Leading zeros are removed** so dates read naturally.

Finally, scoring the call.

```python
def call_score(c):
    score = 2 * sum(1 for s in ["process", "impact", "outcome", "qualify"] if s in c["stages_covered"])
    score += 2 if c["talk_ratio"] <= 0.4 else 0
    score += 2 if c.get("next_step_dated") else 0
    score += 1 if c.get("quotes_captured", 0) >= 2 else 0
    return score, ("good" if score >= 10 else ("fair" if score >= 6 else "redo"))

print(call_score({"stages_covered": ["process", "impact", "outcome", "qualify"], "talk_ratio": 0.15,
                  "next_step_dated": True, "quotes_captured": 2}))
print(call_score({"stages_covered": ["process"], "talk_ratio": 0.75, "next_step_dated": False}))
```

- **The call with Priya scores 13: good.**
- **A pitch-heavy call that only covered the process scores 2:** redo it, starting with the impact and qualification questions.

**Complexity.** Linear apart from the agenda's sort.

BEFORE    choose length -> scale_agenda; pick must-asks per stage; check ICP notes (Module 11.4)

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Rounding agendas carelessly.** Totals drift. Use largest remainders.

**Skipping must-asks in short calls.** Ask them first.

**Writing the summary the next day.** Details fade. Do it within minutes.

**Paraphrasing the problem.** Keep the client's words.

**Empty sections ignored.** Each gap goes to the follow-up.

**Follow-up without a date.** Always date the next step.

**Long follow-up emails.** Three sentences.

**Never scoring calls.** Patterns stay invisible.

**Over-scripting.** The bank is a guide; follow the conversation.

**Forgetting consent to record.** Ask first, Module 11.9.

**Asking about budget last and rushed.** Give qualification its full five minutes.

**Treating "redo" as failure.** It is a prompt for a short second call.
