# Gemini in Google Workspace

The assistants in this course so far have lived in their own window. You bring your work to them: paste the email, upload the spreadsheet, describe the meeting. **Gemini in Google Workspace** reverses the direction. The assistant sits inside Gmail, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Drive, and Meet, where the work already is, and it can read your mail and files for itself.

That removes a great deal of friction. It also means the assistant's answers now depend on what it is permitted to see, which is a question about your organisation's file sharing and not about AI at all. This chapter covers what the assistant does in each application, how it grounds answers in your own material, and what administrators and ordinary users need to understand about privacy and limits.

Feature names and availability in Workspace change constantly and differ by plan, language, and country. Details here are from Google's pages as of November 2026 and from the general shape of the product over the last two years. Treat the feature tables as a map of what to look for, and check your own account.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### A new colleague with your keys

Imagine a new assistant starts work beside you. On the first day they are handed a copy of your keys: they can open every drawer, folder, and mailbox that you can open, and nothing that you cannot. They are quick and willing. They have never worked here before, they sometimes misread what they find, and they will follow written instructions they come across in a document or a letter unless they have been taught to be careful.

Everything important about Gemini in Workspace is in that picture.

- **It works where you work.** No copying and pasting between windows.
- **It sees what you can see.** Its reach is exactly your own permissions.
- **It can be wrong about what it reads.** A summary is a draft.
- **What it reads can contain instructions.** An email or a calendar invitation is text written by someone else.

### Three places you meet it

```text
+------------------------------ A WORKSPACE APP (Docs, Gmail, Sheets ...) -----------------------------+
|                                                                         |                            |
|   your document, mail, or sheet                                         |   SIDE PANEL               |
|                                                                         |   "Summarise this thread"  |
|   [ Help me write ]   <-- 1. INLINE: a button on the thing              |   "What did we agree in    |
|                           you are working on                            |    @Q3-plan about price?"  |
|                                                                         |                            |
|                                                                         |   2. SIDE PANEL: a chat    |
|                                                                         |      about this file and   |
|                                                                         |      your other files      |
+------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

   3. THE GEMINI APP, connected to Workspace: a separate chat that can reach into
      your Gmail, Drive, and Calendar when you ask it to
```

The **inline** tools act on what is in front of you: draft this reply, rewrite this paragraph, fill this column. The **side panel** is a conversation that knows which file is open and can pull in others, which you name with an at-sign. The **Gemini app**, covered in Module 3.10, becomes a third route when it is connected to your Workspace data.

### The shift in what "private" means

Before assistants, a document shared with "anyone in the company with the link" was protected in practice by obscurity. Nobody found it unless someone sent them the link. An assistant that searches on your behalf finds anything you have access to, whether or not you knew it existed. Obscurity stops being a protection. This is the idea in this chapter that organisations most often discover too late, and Section 2 gives it numbers.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### What it does in each application

The following features have been part of Gemini in Workspace. Names and availability vary by plan and change often.

| Application | Typical features | A good first use |
|---|---|---|
| **Gmail** | Draft and refine a message. Summarise a long thread. Suggested replies that use the thread's context. Questions about your inbox in the side panel | "Summarise this thread and list what I have been asked to do" |
| **Docs** | Draft, rewrite, and summarise. Create a formatted document from a prompt and referenced files. Audio summaries of a document | "Draft a project brief using @kickoff-notes and @budget-sheet" |
| **Sheets** | Build a table or tracker from a description. Generate formulas. Analyse data and make charts. A function that applies a prompt to cells | "Which region grew fastest, and chart it" |
| **Slides** | Generate slides and images. Summarise a deck | "Create five slides from @Q3-report for the board" |
| **Drive** | Summarise a file or a folder. Answer questions about a PDF | "What are the payment terms across the contracts in this folder?" |
| **Meet** | Take notes during a call and send a summary with action items. Translated captions | Switching on note-taking for a meeting you would otherwise minute by hand |
| **Chat** | Summarise a conversation you missed | "Catch me up on this space since Monday" |

Two more products sit beside these: a notebook tool for research over your own sources, covered in Module 3.14, and an automation tool for building multi-step flows across the applications.

Since early 2025, Google has included Gemini features in its Business and Enterprise plans for Workspace, where previously they were a separate paid add-on. Personal Google accounts receive similar features through the consumer AI plans described in Module 3.10.

### Grounding in your own files

When you ask the side panel a question, it does not answer from the model's general knowledge alone. It **retrieves** relevant content from your Workspace and places it in the model's context. This is the retrieval described in Module 3.2, with one essential addition: a permission filter.

```text
   your question
        |
        v
   SEARCH your Workspace content
        |
        v
   PERMISSION FILTER: keep only items YOU are allowed to open     <-- the rule that matters
        |
        v
   RANK what remains by relevance, take the top few
        |
        v
   MODEL writes an answer from those items, with links to them
```

Written as a formula, for a user $u$, a question $q$, and the set of all documents $D$:

$$
R(u, q) = \operatorname{top}_k \bigl\{\, d \in D \ : \ \text{access}(u, d) \,\bigr\} \ \text{ranked by } \operatorname{score}(q, d)
$$

The filter comes before the ranking. A document you may not open is never a candidate, however relevant it is. Two colleagues asking the same question can therefore receive different answers, each correct for what that person is allowed to see.

You can direct the retrieval. Naming a file with an at-sign tells the assistant exactly which source to use, which is more reliable than leaving it to search. In the Gemini app, the connection to Gmail, Drive, and Calendar is a setting, and Google's administrator documentation describes it as switched on by default for organisations, requiring conversation history to be on, with changes taking up to a day to apply.

### How access is decided

A file's sharing settings determine who may open it. A simplified model of the rules:

$$
\text{access}(u, d) = \underbrace{u = \text{owner}(d)}_{\text{it is yours}} \ \lor \ \underbrace{u \in S(d)}_{\text{shared with you}} \ \lor \ \underbrace{G(u) \cap S(d) \ne \emptyset}_{\text{shared with your group}} \ \lor \ \underbrace{\text{link}(d) = \text{domain} \land \text{dom}(u) = \text{dom}(d)}_{\text{anyone in the organisation}} \ \lor \ \underbrace{\text{link}(d) = \text{public}}_{\text{anyone at all}}
$$

where $S(d)$ is the set of people and groups the file is shared with and $G(u)$ is the set of groups the user belongs to. The assistant does not have its own permissions. It borrows yours.

### Oversharing, with numbers

The last two terms of that formula are the dangerous ones. "Anyone in the organisation with the link" was a convenient setting when finding a file required the link. With an assistant, every such file is one question away from every employee.

Suppose an organisation has $N$ sensitive files and a fraction $s$ of them have been shared organisation-wide. The number that any employee's assistant can read is

$$
E = N \cdot s
$$

For 2,000 sensitive files and $s = 0.03$, that is 60 files: salary spreadsheets, draft contracts, or performance reviews that an assistant may quote in answer to an innocent question.

What changes with an assistant is the probability of discovery. A file is surfaced if it is accessible and it is retrieved:

$$
P(\text{surfaced}) = P(\text{accessible}) \times P(\text{retrieved} \mid \text{accessible, relevant})
$$

The first factor has not changed. The second used to be close to zero, since someone had to stumble on the link, and is now high for any relevant question. Nothing about the permissions is different. The consequences of the permissions are.

This is why Google's guidance, and that of every vendor of such assistants, is to review sharing before or alongside switching the assistant on. The assistant does not create the exposure. It reveals it.

### Privacy commitments for organisations

Consumer and organisational accounts are governed by different terms. Module 3.10 described the consumer position, where saved chats may be reviewed and used to improve models. For Workspace customers, Google's Privacy Hub for generative AI states that customer content is not reviewed by people or used to train generative AI models outside the customer's domain without permission. The commitment is written into the service terms, and the page lists the editions it applies to.

For a user this means a work or school account is the right place for work content, and a personal account is not. For an administrator it means reading the page for your own edition, since exclusions exist.

### Admin controls

Administrators decide where the assistant is available. Google's admin documentation describes settings to switch Gemini features on or off for individual services, and a separate setting for whether the Gemini app may reach Workspace data.

Such settings follow the structure of the organisation. An organisation is arranged as a tree of **organisational units**: the whole company at the root, departments below, teams below those. A setting applied to a unit is inherited by every unit beneath it, unless a lower unit sets its own.

```text
   company            Gemini in Gmail: ON
      |
      +-- sales                 (inherits ON)
      |     +-- sales-emea      (inherits ON)
      |
      +-- legal       Gemini in Gmail: OFF
            +-- legal-litigation  (inherits OFF)
            +-- legal-contracts   Gemini in Gmail: ON   (its own setting)
```

The effective setting for a unit is the one on the nearest ancestor that has set it, the unit itself included. This rule is worth understanding because it explains the commonest support question in any organisation: "why does my colleague have this feature and I do not?"

### Text that gives orders

The assistant reads emails, documents, and calendar entries written by other people, and Module 1.10 explained that a model cannot perfectly separate content from instructions. In Workspace this is a concrete risk and has been demonstrated.

At a security conference in August 2025, researchers presented attacks in which a calendar invitation containing hidden instructions caused Gemini, when later asked about the user's schedule, to carry out actions the user never requested. The reported effects included deleting calendar events, producing harmful links, and leaking the subjects of emails. The researchers had disclosed the problem to Google months earlier, and Google introduced defences that included asking for confirmation before sensitive actions, sanitising links, and classifiers that detect injected instructions.

The lesson is the lethal trifecta of Module 3.6. An assistant in your mailbox has private data, reads untrusted content, and can act. Vendors' defences reduce the risk. Your own habits finish the job: read confirmation prompts, and be suspicious of an assistant that suddenly proposes an action unrelated to what you asked.

### Summaries are drafts

A thread summary is generated text. It can omit the one message that mattered, attribute a statement to the wrong person, or include a figure that appears nowhere in the thread. Users have reported exactly these. The check is the one from Module 3.9: for anything you will act on, look at the source. A quick mechanical test catches the crudest errors, which is whether every number and name in the summary appears in the original.

### Writing prompts for Workspace

Google's prompting guide for Workspace recommends the four elements introduced in Module 3.11: persona, task, context, and format. In Workspace the context element has a specific form, which is naming files.

```text
You are a project manager.                               (persona)
Draft a status update for the steering group.            (task)
Use @sprint-12-notes and @risk-register.                 (context)
Three short paragraphs, then a table of open risks.      (format)
```

The guide's own advice is that not every prompt needs all four, and that a clear verb for the task matters most.

### Complexity

A permission check is constant time per file with sets. Permission-filtered search is linear in the number of files a naive search examines. An audit of sharing is linear in the number of files. Resolving an inherited setting takes as many steps as the unit is deep in the tree.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

The permission model can be built in a few lines, and building it makes the behaviour of the assistant predictable. First, access and permission-filtered search.

```python
STOPWORDS = {"a", "an", "and", "are", "for", "in", "is", "of", "on", "our", "the", "to", "what", "who"}

def keywords(text):
    words = {word.strip(".,;:!?()\"'").lower() for word in text.split()}
    return words - STOPWORDS - {""}

GROUPS = {"asha": {"finance"}, "ben": {"sales"}, "chen": {"sales", "managers"}}

FILES = [
    {"name": "sales-playbook", "owner": "chen", "shared": {"sales"}, "link": "restricted", "domain": "acme.example",
     "text": "Discount policy for sales: up to 10 percent without approval."},
    {"name": "salary-review-2026", "owner": "asha", "shared": set(), "link": "domain", "domain": "acme.example",
     "text": "Salary review: proposed salary bands and discount to budget for each manager."},
    {"name": "board-minutes", "owner": "asha", "shared": {"managers"}, "link": "restricted", "domain": "acme.example",
     "text": "Board minutes: approved the acquisition and the new discount policy."},
    {"name": "press-kit", "owner": "ben", "shared": set(), "link": "public", "domain": "acme.example",
     "text": "Press kit with logos and company description."},
]

def can_access(user, domain, file, groups=GROUPS):
    return (user == file["owner"]
            or user in file["shared"]
            or bool(groups.get(user, set()) & file["shared"])
            or (file["link"] == "domain" and domain == file["domain"])
            or file["link"] == "public")

def grounded_search(user, domain, query, files, groups=GROUPS, k=3):
    wanted = keywords(query)
    allowed = [file for file in files if can_access(user, domain, file, groups)]
    scored = [(len(wanted & keywords(file["text"])), file["name"]) for file in allowed]
    ranked = sorted((item for item in scored if item[0] > 0), key=lambda item: (-item[0], item[1]))
    return [name for _, name in ranked[:k]]

for user in ("ben", "chen", "asha"):
    print(f"{user:>5}: {grounded_search(user, 'acme.example', 'What is our discount policy?', FILES)}")
print("guest:", grounded_search("dana", "partner.example", "What is our discount policy?", FILES))
```

- **`can_access` is the five-way rule from Section 2.** Owner, shared directly, shared through a group, shared with the whole domain, or public. The assistant's reach is this function evaluated for you.
- **`grounded_search` filters before it ranks.** The list `allowed` is built first. A file the user cannot open never receives a score, so it cannot appear however well it matches.
- **The same question gets three different answers.** Ben, in sales, gets the playbook. Chen, a manager in sales, also gets the board minutes. Asha, in finance, cannot see the sales playbook and gets the minutes she owns.
- **Everyone inside the company also gets the salary review.** It is shared with "anyone in the organisation", and it happens to mention a discount. Nobody was sent that link. A question about discount policy surfaced it. This is oversharing made visible.
- **The outside guest gets nothing.** The only file open to someone outside the domain is the public press kit, which is not relevant. The domain-wide setting stops at the organisation's edge.

Next, the audit that should run before the assistant is switched on, and the summary check.

```python
SENSITIVE = ("salary", "payroll", "contract", "acquisition", "password", "medical")

def oversharing_audit(files, sensitive=SENSITIVE):
    findings = []
    for file in files:
        if file["link"] in ("domain", "public"):
            haystack = (file["name"] + " " + file["text"]).lower()
            if any(word in haystack for word in sensitive):
                findings.append((file["name"], file["link"] + "-sensitive"))
    return findings

def unsupported_terms(summary, source):
    def terms(text):
        cleaned = [word.strip(".,;:!?()\"'") for word in text.split()]
        return {word.lower() for word in cleaned if word and (word[0].isupper() or any(ch.isdigit() for ch in word))}
    return sorted(terms(summary) - {word.strip(".,;:!?()\"'").lower() for word in source.split()})

print(oversharing_audit(FILES))
THREAD = "Priya: The supplier quoted 4,800 for the server. Marco: Approved, but delivery must be before 12 March."
print(unsupported_terms("Priya reported a quote of 4,800. Marco approved it for delivery before 12 March.", THREAD))
print(unsupported_terms("Priya reported a quote of 5,800. Sanjay approved it for delivery before 21 March.", THREAD))
```

- **The audit looks only at widely shared files.** A sensitive file shared with two named people is working as intended. The combination to find is sensitive content with a domain-wide or public link.
- **The salary review is found.** It is the single finding, labelled with how widely it is shared. On a real drive this list is the work to do before rollout.
- **The keyword list is a starting point.** Real audits use the labels and classifications the organisation already has. A crude scan still finds the worst cases, which are usually named exactly what they are.
- **`unsupported_terms` collects names and numbers.** A word counts if it starts with a capital letter or contains a digit. These are the parts of a summary that are easiest to get wrong and most damaging when wrong.
- **The faithful summary returns nothing.** Every name and figure in it appears in the thread.
- **The second summary returns three terms.** The amount is wrong, the date is wrong, and the approver is someone who is not in the thread at all. A check like this cannot tell whether a summary is complete or fair. It catches invented specifics.

Finally, the admin setting and its inheritance.

```python
PARENTS = {"sales": "company", "sales-emea": "sales", "legal": "company",
           "legal-litigation": "legal", "legal-contracts": "legal"}
SETTINGS = {"company": {"gmail": True, "meet": True}, "legal": {"gmail": False}, "legal-contracts": {"gmail": True}}

def gemini_enabled(unit, service, settings=SETTINGS, parents=PARENTS):
    while unit is not None:
        if service in settings.get(unit, {}):
            return settings[unit][service]
        unit = parents.get(unit)
    return False

for unit in ("sales-emea", "legal-litigation", "legal-contracts"):
    print(f"{unit:<17} gmail: {gemini_enabled(unit, 'gmail')}   meet: {gemini_enabled(unit, 'meet')}   sheets: {gemini_enabled(unit, 'sheets')}")
```

- **The loop walks up the tree.** It starts at the unit and asks whether a setting for this service exists here. If not, it moves to the parent and asks again.
- **The nearest setting wins.** The litigation team inherits "off" from legal. The contracts team has its own "on", which overrides legal's. Both inherit "on" for Meet from the company, because legal set nothing for Meet.
- **No setting anywhere means off.** Nobody has configured Sheets, so the answer is false for every unit. A feature that reaches people's data should be enabled by a decision, not by an omission.

**Complexity.** `can_access` is constant time with sets. `grounded_search` and `oversharing_audit` are linear in the number of files. `gemini_enabled` takes at most as many steps as the tree is deep.

### A rollout checklist for administrators

1. **Audit sharing first.** Find sensitive content shared organisation-wide or publicly, and fix it.
2. **Decide by unit.** Start with a pilot group. Leave it off where the data is most sensitive until the audit is done.
3. **Read the Privacy Hub for your edition.** Confirm which commitments apply to you.
4. **Decide about the Gemini app.** It is a separate setting from the features inside the applications.
5. **Tell people what it can see.** "It can read everything you can read" prevents most surprises.
6. **Teach the confirmation habit.** Prompts before sending, deleting, or sharing are there to be read.

### Habits for users

- **Name your sources.** Use the at-sign to point at the file you mean.
- **Ask it which files it used**, and open them.
- **Treat every summary as a draft.** Check figures, names, and dates before you act or forward.
- **Use your work account for work.** The terms are different.
- **Be wary of surprises.** If the assistant proposes an action you did not ask for, stop and look at what it has just read.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**"It showed me a file I should not have seen."** The file was shared with the whole organisation, usually years ago and by accident. The assistant respected the permission exactly. Report it, and the owner should fix the sharing. Administrators who audit before rollout avoid a wave of such reports.

**Summaries with invented details.** Users have reported email summaries containing content that was not in the messages. A summary that will lead to an action, a payment, or a reply to a customer needs a glance at the original.

**"It read my document without being asked."** In July 2024 a user reported that Gemini had summarised a private tax document in Drive that he had not asked about, and the story was widely covered. Google said the assistant reads content only when the user starts an interaction. Whatever the cause, the episode shows how unsettling ambient assistance can be. Know where the settings are, and expect the assistant to act on the file that is open.

**Features that are not there.** A tutorial shows a button you do not have. The cause is usually the plan, the language, the country, a gradual rollout, or an administrator's setting. Check those before concluding you are doing something wrong.

**Two sets of controls.** Administrators report confusion between the settings for Gemini inside the applications and the separate settings for the Gemini app's access to Workspace data. Switching off one does not switch off the other.

**Wrong formulas in Sheets.** A generated formula can be syntactically valid and logically wrong: the wrong range, a count where a sum was wanted. Test it on a few rows whose answer you can work out by hand, as you would any generated code under Module 3.8.

**Vague prompts in the side panel.** "Summarise my emails" produces a vague result. "Summarise emails from the Acme team since Monday and list what I have been asked to do" produces a useful one. The four elements of the prompting guide apply.

**Meeting notes that miss the point.** Automatic notes capture what was said. They can miss what was decided, especially when the decision was implicit. Read the notes once after the meeting and correct the action items while you still remember.

**Instructions hidden in incoming content.** The calendar-invitation attacks showed that text arriving from outside can steer the assistant. Do not approve an action you did not request, and treat invitations and emails from unknown senders with the usual caution.

**Confidential work in a personal account.** People use their personal Google account for work because it is the one that is signed in. Consumer terms apply there, including possible human review. Check which account is active.

**Assuming it replaces filing.** An assistant that can find anything tempts people to stop organising. Retrieval works better on well-named files in sensible folders, and so do your colleagues.

**Encrypted and restricted content.** Files protected with additional encryption, or by rules that restrict downloading and copying, may be unavailable to the assistant by design. If it says it cannot read a file you can open, that may be a control working as intended.

## 5. Active Recall Check & Synthesis Summary

**Summary.** Gemini in Google Workspace places the assistant inside the applications where work happens, as inline tools, a side panel that can draw on your files, and a connection from the Gemini app to your mail, drive, and calendar. It grounds answers in your own content through retrieval that is filtered by your permissions before anything is ranked, so the assistant sees exactly what you can see. That makes existing oversharing visible: files shared organisation-wide, once protected by obscurity, become one question away from every employee. For organisations, Google commits that content is not reviewed by people or used to train models outside the domain without permission, and administrators control availability through settings inherited down the tree of organisational units. The assistant reads text written by others, which has been shown to carry injected instructions, and its summaries are drafts whose names, numbers, and dates should be checked. The effective rollout order is to fix sharing, enable by unit, and teach users what the assistant can see.

Explain why the risk is greater after the assistant is switched on even though no permission has changed, using the two-factor expression from Section 2.

<summary>View Answer</summary>

Two percent of 5,000 is 100 files. Any employee's assistant can read all 100, because the assistant has that employee's permissions and the files are open to everyone in the organisation. The chance that a given file is surfaced is the chance that it is accessible multiplied by the chance that it is retrieved given that it is accessible and relevant. The first factor is unchanged by the assistant: those 100 files were accessible to every employee yesterday too. What has changed is the second factor.     allowed = [file for file in files if can_access(user, domain, file, groups)]
    scored = [(len(wanted & keywords(file["text"])), file["name"]) for file in allowed]
    ranked = sorted((item for item in scored if item[0] > 0), key=lambda item: (-item[0], item[1]))
    return [name for _, name in ranked[:k]]
