# ChatGPT for Business

Using ChatGPT yourself and deploying it across a company are different problems. The first is about prompts. The second is about data protection, access control, choosing the right work to apply it to, training people, and proving that it paid for itself. Many organisations have bought licences and seen little change. This chapter covers what the business plans provide, what the evidence says about productivity, and how to run a rollout that produces a measurable result. It applies equally if you are advising a client as a freelancer or consultant, which is where many readers of this course will meet these questions.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### The café table and the office

Picture two ways of working with the same excellent assistant.

At a **café table**, you chat with the assistant in a public place. It is convenient and cheap. There is no lock, no record of who came and went, and the café's house rules, which you did not write, govern what happens to anything left on the table.

In a **company office**, the assistant works inside your building. There is a door with a lock, a reception desk that knows who is allowed in, a log of activity, and a contract that says nothing leaves the building.

A personal ChatGPT account is the café table. A business workspace is the office. The assistant, the underlying model, is the same in both. What you are paying for in a business plan is the building.

### Seats are not results

The second idea is about where value comes from. A common pattern is to buy a licence for every employee and wait for productivity to rise. Usually it does not, for a simple reason: a tool creates value only when it changes how a specific piece of work gets done.

Think of a gym membership. Buying it produces no fitness. A plan, a routine, and measurement do. In the same way, value from AI comes from a **workflow**: a particular, repeated task, with an owner, a defined way of using the tool, and a before-and-after measurement. A rollout that starts from workflows succeeds far more often than one that starts from seats.

### The jagged frontier

The third idea comes from research, covered in Section 2. AI's abilities are uneven in a way that is hard to predict. It is excellent at some tasks and poor at others that look equally difficult. Researchers call the boundary the **jagged frontier**.

```text
            tasks AI does well                tasks AI does badly
   ......................................|.............................
   draft an email    summarise a report  |  check a subtle legal point
   brainstorm names  reformat data       |  judge a client's real intent
   write a first     explain a concept   |  reconcile accounts exactly
   proposal draft                        |  notice what is missing
                                         ^
                      the frontier is jagged, not straight, and
                      staff cannot see it without experience
```

Inside the frontier, AI makes people faster and better. Outside it, people who trust the AI do worse than people working alone. The practical consequence is that a rollout must teach staff where the edge is for their own work, and must keep human review on anything near it.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### What the business plans add

At the time of writing, OpenAI offers ChatGPT Business (previously called Team) and ChatGPT Enterprise. Prices and exact feature lists change, and third-party sources disagree on details, so confirm specifics during procurement. The durable differences from a personal account are these.

| Capability | What it means | Why a business cares |
|---|---|---|
| No training on your data by default | Conversations are not used to improve models | Client and company information stays out of future models |
| Shared workspace | Projects, files, and assistants can be shared within the organisation | Work becomes reusable instead of trapped in one person's account |
| Single sign-on (SSO) | Staff log in with the company identity system | Access ends automatically when someone leaves |
| Admin console | Manage members, permissions, and which features are enabled | Central control |
| Connectors | Link to company storage, mail, and chat | The assistant can work with real company documents |
| Compliance attestations | Independent security audits such as SOC 2 | Required by many clients and regulators |
| Enterprise additions | Automated user provisioning, audit logs, data-retention and residency controls, encryption key management | Needed in regulated or large organisations |

Two cautions are worth carrying into any purchasing discussion. Business and Enterprise are not the same product with different prices: several controls that auditors ask about exist only on Enterprise. And a connector exposes to the assistant whatever the user can already reach, so an organisation with untidy file permissions will discover that when the assistant starts surfacing documents people should not have been able to see.

### What the evidence says about productivity

Four studies are cited constantly. Know what each measured.

| Study | Setting | Result | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, "Generative AI at Work" | About 5,000 customer-support agents given an AI assistant | Issues resolved per hour rose 14 percent on average, and 34 percent for novice and low-skilled workers | Almost no gain for the most experienced agents |
| Noy & Zhang (*Science*, 2023) | 453 professionals doing short writing tasks | Time fell by about 40 percent and quality rose by about 18 percent | Short, self-contained tasks in an online experiment |
| Dell'Acqua and colleagues with Boston Consulting Group (2023) | 758 consultants on realistic consulting tasks | Inside the frontier: 12 percent more tasks, 25 percent faster, over 40 percent higher quality | Outside the frontier: 19 percentage points less likely to be correct than those without AI |
| MIT NANDA, "The GenAI Divide" (2025) | Interviews, a survey, and a review of deployments | Headline claim that 95 percent of enterprise pilots show no measurable return | Widely criticised: small unnamed sample, undisclosed data, a narrow definition of success |

Read together, they tell a consistent story. The gains are real and can be large. They are concentrated in particular tasks and in less experienced workers. They can turn negative when the tool is used on the wrong work. And at the level of whole organisations, gains often fail to show up, because the tool was added without changing any workflow. Vendor reports of time saved, such as self-reported figures of 40 to 60 minutes a day, are surveys of opinion and should be treated as upper bounds.

### The return-on-investment calculation

A defensible business case fits in one formula. Let

- $N$ be the number of seats,
- $a$ the **adoption rate**, the fraction of seats in real regular use,
- $h$ the hours saved per active user per month,
- $r$ the loaded hourly cost of that person's time,
- $o$ the **overhead fraction**, the share of saved time spent reviewing and correcting AI output,
- $c$ the seat price per month,
- $T$ the one-off cost of setup and training.

The net monthly benefit is

$$
B = N \times a \times h \times r \times (1 - o) \; - \; N \times c
$$

The **payback period**, the number of months to recover the one-off cost, is

$$
\text{payback} = \frac{T}{B} \qquad (B > 0)
$$

and return on investment over a period is

$$
\text{ROI} = \frac{\text{benefit} - \text{cost}}{\text{cost}}
$$

**Worked example.** Twenty seats at USD 25 each, 60 percent adoption, 6 hours saved per active user per month, a loaded cost of USD 20 per hour, 25 percent overhead, and USD 2,000 of training.

$$
B = 20 \times 0.6 \times 6 \times 20 \times 0.75 - 20 \times 25 = 1080 - 500 = 580
$$

Payback is $2000 / 580 \approx 3.4$ months. Now halve the adoption rate to 30 percent, which is common when nobody owns the rollout:

$$
B = 20 \times 0.3 \times 6 \times 20 \times 0.75 - 500 = 540 - 500 = 40
$$

Payback becomes 50 months. Nothing about the tool changed. **Adoption and overhead, the two variables that depend on training and workflow design, decide whether the investment pays.** Notice also what the formula leaves out: saved hours are worth $r$ only if they are put to productive use, and research on this point suggests much of the saved time is absorbed into other tasks without showing up as revenue.

### Choosing where to start

Not every use case deserves effort. Score each candidate from 1 to 5 on three questions.

- **Value.** How much time or money does this work consume, and how often is it done?
- **Feasibility.** Is the task inside the frontier, is the data available, can the output be checked?
- **Risk.** What does an error cost, and how sensitive is the data?

A simple priority score is

$$
\text{score} = \frac{\text{value} \times \text{feasibility}}{\text{risk}}
$$

High-value, feasible, low-risk tasks rise to the top. Typical high scorers are first drafts of routine documents, summarising meetings and long reports, answering internal questions from company documents, and reformatting or categorising data. Typical low scorers are anything where an undetected error is expensive and checking is hard: final legal language, financial figures that will be relied on, decisions about individual people.

By function, the common starting points are:

| Function | Good first use | Keep a human on |
|---|---|---|
| Customer support | Drafting replies from the knowledge base | Refunds, complaints, anything unusual |
| Sales | Researching a prospect, drafting outreach | Pricing, commitments, contract terms |
| Marketing | First drafts, variations, repurposing content | Claims about the product, brand voice sign-off |
| Operations | Summarising documents, writing procedures | Anything safety-related or contractual |
| Analysis | Exploring data, drafting commentary | Every reported number, per Module 2.8 |

### The policy

A rollout needs a short written policy before it needs anything else. The core is **data classification**: sorting information into classes and stating which tools each class may enter.

| Class | Examples | Where it may go |
|---|---|---|
| Public | Published marketing, public web content | Any tool |
| Internal | Process documents, internal memos | Company-approved tools |
| Confidential | Client files, contracts, financial records | Business workspace only |
| Regulated | Personal identity numbers, payment card data, health records | Not without specific approval |

The rest of an acceptable-use policy covers a list of approved tools, a requirement that a person reviews anything sent outside the company, rules on disclosing AI use where clients require it, a route for reporting incidents, a named owner, and a review date. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework's four functions, govern, map, measure, manage, give a ready-made structure if a client asks for one.

The reason the policy comes first is **shadow AI**: staff using personal accounts for work because no approved option exists. Banning AI does not stop this. Providing an approved tool and clear rules does.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

The formulas from Section 2 become a small planning tool.

```python
def net_monthly_benefit(seats, adoption, hours_saved, hourly_cost, overhead, seat_price):
    """Value of time saved by active users, after review overhead, minus licence cost."""
    gross = seats * adoption * hours_saved * hourly_cost * (1 - overhead)
    return gross - seats * seat_price

def payback_months(one_off_cost, net_monthly):
    """Months to recover the one-off cost. None if the rollout never pays back."""
    if net_monthly <= 0:
        return None
    return one_off_cost / net_monthly

base = dict(seats=20, hours_saved=6, hourly_cost=20, seat_price=25)
print("adoption  overhead   net/month   payback")
for adoption in (0.3, 0.6, 0.9):
    for overhead in (0.25, 0.5):
        net = net_monthly_benefit(adoption=adoption, overhead=overhead, **base)
        months = payback_months(2000, net)
        shown = f"{months:5.1f} months" if months else "never"
        print(f"   {adoption:.1f}      {overhead:.2f}    {net:8.0f}   {shown}")
```

- **`net_monthly_benefit` mirrors the formula term by term.** Keeping the code in the same shape as the equation makes it easy to check, and easy for a client to follow.
- **`payback_months` returns `None` when the net benefit is zero or negative.** Dividing would give a negative or infinite number of months, which is meaningless. `None` says plainly that the investment is never recovered.
- **The table is a sensitivity analysis.** A business case built on one set of assumptions invites the question "what if you are wrong?" Varying adoption and overhead answers it in advance. In this example the rollout pays back in about two months at 90 percent adoption and 25 percent overhead, takes 50 months at 30 percent adoption, and never pays back at all if adoption is 30 percent and half the saved time goes on corrections.
- **`**base` passes the fixed assumptions.** Holding some inputs constant while varying others is the code equivalent of changing one thing at a time.

Next, prioritising use cases.

```python
def prioritise(use_cases):
    """Names ordered by value * feasibility / risk, highest first; ties by name."""
    scored = [(-(u["value"] * u["feasibility"] / u["risk"]), u["name"]) for u in use_cases]
    return [name for _, name in sorted(scored)]

candidates = [
    {"name": "Draft client email replies", "value": 4, "feasibility": 5, "risk": 2},
    {"name": "Summarise meeting notes", "value": 3, "feasibility": 5, "risk": 1},
    {"name": "Prepare final tax filings", "value": 5, "feasibility": 2, "risk": 5},
    {"name": "Categorise expense receipts", "value": 4, "feasibility": 4, "risk": 2},
]
for rank, name in enumerate(prioritise(candidates), start=1):
    print(rank, name)
```

The highest-value item on the list, tax filings, comes last. Its value is 5, but feasibility is low and an error is costly, giving a score of 2. Summarising meeting notes, the least glamorous item, comes first with a score of 15. That reversal is the main lesson of use-case selection: start with what is safe and checkable, and earn the right to tackle the valuable, risky work once people know the tool.

Finally, the data-classification gate.

```python
import re

POLICY = {"public": {"personal", "business", "enterprise"},
          "confidential": {"business", "enterprise"},
          "regulated": set()}

def classify(text):
    """Highest data class detected in a piece of text."""
    if re.search(r"\b[A-Z]{5}[0-9]{4}[A-Z]\b", text) or re.search(r"\b\d(?:[ -]?\d){11,15}\b", text):
        return "regulated"
    if re.search(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.-]+", text) or re.search(r"\b(confidential|salary|password)\b", text, re.I):
        return "confidential"
    return "public"

def gate(text, tool):
    return "allow" if tool in POLICY[classify(text)] else "block"

samples = ["Our office is open 9 to 5 on weekdays.",
           "Salary review for the Pune team is attached.",
           "Client PAN is ABCDE1234F, please file by Friday."]
for text in samples:
    print(f"{classify(text):>12} | personal: {gate(text, 'personal'):5} | business: {gate(text, 'business'):5} | {text}")
```

- **The checks run from most to least sensitive.** A message containing both an email address and an identity number is regulated, and must be reported as such.
- **The first pattern matches the format of an Indian PAN** (five letters, four digits, one letter). The second matches any run of 12 to 16 digits, allowing spaces or hyphens, which covers payment card numbers and 12-digit identity numbers. Patterns like these are illustrative. A real policy would add the identifiers relevant to its country and sector.
- **`POLICY` maps each class to the set of tools that may receive it.** The regulated class maps to an empty set: nothing is allowed without specific approval. Policy as data, separate from the code that applies it, means the rules can be changed without touching the logic.
- **Its limits must be stated.** Pattern matching detects formats. It cannot tell that "the Sharma file" is a client matter or that a paragraph describes an unannounced acquisition. A gate like this catches accidents and supports a policy. It does not replace judgement or training.

**Complexity.** The calculators are constant time. Prioritising sorts $n$ use cases in time proportional to $n \log n$. Classification is linear in the length of the text.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Licences without workflows produce nothing.** The most common failure is a company-wide purchase with no named owner, no chosen tasks, and no measurement. Six months later usage is concentrated in a few enthusiasts. Start with one or two workflows and a baseline measurement.

**Self-reported time savings are not ROI.** "I think it saves me an hour a day" is an impression. Measure something countable before and after: documents completed, tickets resolved, turnaround time. Expect the measured figure to be lower.

**Review time eats the gain.** If output needs heavy correction, the net saving shrinks or reverses. This is the overhead term in the formula, and it is highest in exactly the work that is closest to the frontier. Pick tasks where checking is quick.

**Experienced staff gain least and should not be the benchmark.** The support-agent study found the largest gains for newcomers. Your senior people may see little benefit in their own work and still benefit from their juniors getting up to speed faster.

**Personal accounts for client work are the default risk.** In the absence of an approved tool, people use what they have. A freelancer or employee pasting client documents into a personal account may be breaching a contract or a confidentiality agreement without realising it. Check the terms you have signed.

**Business is not Enterprise.** Teams discover during a client's security review that the plan they bought lacks audit logs, automated provisioning, or residency controls. Establish the requirements before choosing the plan.

**Fix file permissions before turning on connectors.** An assistant connected to shared storage will find documents that were technically accessible and practically hidden. Clean up access first.

**Headline statistics travel without their caveats.** "AI makes workers 40 percent more productive" and "95 percent of AI pilots fail" are both quoted far beyond what their sources support. When you present a business case, cite the setting and the limits along with the number. Clients trust the consultant who does.

**Train on the failures, not just the features.** Staff need to see the tool being confidently wrong on their own kind of work. Training that shows only impressive demos produces the over-trust that makes outcomes worse outside the frontier.

**Plan for change.** Products, prices, and features shift every few months, as the retirement of custom GPTs in Module 2.6 shows. Keep your instructions, templates, and reference documents in portable form, and avoid building anything critical on a feature you cannot replace.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

**Question 3.** A freelance bookkeeper uses a personal ChatGPT account and wants to paste in a client's bank statement to categorise transactions. Using the data-classification table, explain the problem and describe two acceptable ways to get the job done.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

A bank statement contains account numbers and transaction details that identify the client: it is confidential and may include regulated data. Under the policy table, confidential data may go only into a business workspace, and regulated data nowhere without specific approval, so a personal account is not permitted. Beyond policy, a personal account may allow the data to be used for model training unless settings are changed, and the freelancer's contract or confidentiality agreement with the client may forbid sharing the data with third parties at all. Two acceptable approaches: first, with the client's written permission, use a business workspace where data is not used for training, after removing account numbers and other identifiers the task does not need; second, avoid sending the real data at all, by asking the assistant to write a categorisation script or spreadsheet formula using made-up sample rows and then running that on the real statement locally.

def net_monthly_benefit(seats, adoption, hours_saved, hourly_cost, overhead, seat_price):
    """The code has the same shape as the formula, so a client can check it term by term.

    Adoption and overhead depend on training and workflow design, and they matter far
    more to the result than the seat price does.
    """
    gross = seats * adoption * hours_saved * hourly_cost * (1 - overhead)
    return gross - seats * seat_price


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 2
def payback_months(one_off_cost, net_monthly):
    """None says plainly that the investment is never recovered."""
    if net_monthly <= 0:
        return None
    return one_off_cost / net_monthly


def roi(benefit, cost):
    """Return on investment as a fraction: 0.5 means a 50 percent return."""
    return (benefit - cost) / cost


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 3
def prioritise(use_cases):
    """Start with what is valuable, feasible, AND safe. High value alone is not enough."""
    scored = [(-(case["value"] * case["feasibility"] / case["risk"]), case["name"])
              for case in use_cases]
    return [name for _, name in sorted(scored)]


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 4
def classify(text):
    """Check from most to least sensitive so the HIGHEST class present is reported.

    Patterns detect formats. They cannot recognise that 'the Sharma file' is a client
    matter. A gate like this catches accidents; it does not replace judgement.
    """
    if re.search(PAN_PATTERN, text) or re.search(LONG_NUMBER_PATTERN, text):
        return "regulated"
    if re.search(EMAIL_PATTERN, text) or re.search(KEYWORD_PATTERN, text, re.I):
        return "confidential"
    return "public"


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 5
def gate(text, tool):
    """Policy is data (POLICY); this function only applies it."""
    return "allow" if tool in POLICY[classify(text)] else "block"
