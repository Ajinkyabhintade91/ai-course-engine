# Client Proposal Template

Module 11.8 explained how proposals work: they confirm the discovery call and ask for a decision. This module gives you the **template** itself, ready to fill in, with **guidance for each section** and **variations** for small and large projects. A template saves time, but more importantly it makes proposals consistent: every client gets the same clear structure, nothing important is forgotten, and you can improve the template once and benefit on every future proposal.

Prices and dates continue the Harper Joinery example and are illustrative.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### The joiner's printed quote form

An established joiner does not write each quote on a blank sheet. They use a printed form with boxes: customer, job description, materials, exclusions, dates, price, deposit, and signature. The form guarantees that every quote covers the same ground, and the joiner's attention goes into what is different about this job, not into remembering what to include. For a small job, some boxes stay short; for a large one, extra pages are attached: drawings, phases, conditions. The proposal template works the same way.

```text
 SMALL (under GBP 3,000)          MEDIUM (GBP 3,000 to 15,000)          LARGE (GBP 15,000 and above)
 problem                          problem                               problem
 solution                         solution                              solution
 scope                            scope                                 scope
 timeline                         timeline                              phases
 price                            assumptions                           timeline
 next step                        risks                                 assumptions, risks
 (about 700 words)                price, next step  (about 930 words)    acceptance criteria, governance
                                                                        price, next step (about 1,330 words)
 fill {placeholders} from discovery notes -> check none are left -> validity date, deposit, balance
```

### Fill, then think

The template carries the structure; you supply the content from the discovery notes, Module 11.9. Any field you cannot fill is a sign the discovery was incomplete: go back and ask, rather than guessing in writing.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### The template

```text
Proposal for {client}
PROBLEM: "{problem_quote}". This costs about GBP {annual_cost} a year.
SOLUTION: {outcome}
SCOPE: {deliverables}. Not included: {exclusions}.
TIMELINE: {timeline}
PRICE: {price_options}
NEXT STEP: {next_step}. Valid until {valid_until}.
```

Each placeholder in braces is filled from the discovery notes and the pricing work.

### Section-by-section guidance

**Problem.** Quote the client's own words, then state the cost you measured together, Module 11.3. Two or three sentences. Do not introduce anything the client did not say.

**Solution.** Lead with the outcome, "same-day quotes", then one or two sentences on how. Avoid technical terms the client did not use.

**Scope.** List deliverables as checkable items, the number of revision rounds, and exclusions in plain language, Module 11.6. This is the section that prevents disputes.

**Timeline.** Milestones with business-day dates, Module 11.8, assuming an immediate start, with a note that dates move with the start.

**Price.** Three options, highest first, each with its first-year return and payback, the recommended option marked, and payment terms, Module 11.7.

**Next step.** One action and a validity date: "Sign and pay the deposit; valid until 1 December 2026."

### Variations by size

Larger projects carry more risk for both sides, so their proposals add sections:

- **Assumptions:** what you assume to be true, such as access to data by a date or a named contact's availability. If an assumption fails, the timeline or price may change.
- **Risks:** known risks and how they will be handled, such as data quality or a third-party API.
- **Phases:** for large projects, the work split into phases, each with its own deliverables and payment; a discovery phase first reduces risk for everyone.
- **Acceptance criteria:** how each deliverable will be judged complete, such as the success measure, Module 11.11.
- **Governance:** how decisions are made, who approves changes, and how often progress is reviewed.

A simple rule chooses sections by total price: under GBP 3,000, the six core sections; from GBP 3,000 to under GBP 15,000, add assumptions and risks; at GBP 15,000 and above, add phases, acceptance criteria, and governance as well. The thresholds are a starting point; adjust them to your work. For large projects, a separate **statement of work**, a formal document describing the work, deliverables, and terms, often accompanies or replaces the proposal's scope section.

### Word budgets

Proposals should be short. Indicative budgets per section, problem 120 words, solution 150, scope 200, timeline 80, price 120, next step 40, assumptions 100, risks 120, phases 200, acceptance criteria 120, governance 80, give about 700 words for a small proposal, 930 for a medium one, and 1,330 for a large one, before any appendices.

### Validity and payment terms

A proposal should expire, so that prices and schedules can be revisited: 14 days for small projects and 30 for large ones are common. The deposit is a share of the total, with the balance due on completion; large projects usually replace this with **phase payments**:

$$\text{deposit} = d \times \text{total}, \qquad \text{balance} = \text{total} - \text{deposit}$$

and a running total of phase prices shows the client's cumulative commitment at each milestone.

### A worked example: Priya's proposal, section by section

Applied to Harper Joinery, the guidance produces a short document. **Problem:** "Quotes take three hours" and "we lose jobs to faster firms"; pricing materials alone costs about GBP 21,312 a year in time and errors. **Solution:** same-day quotes, through a pricing assistant that drafts line items from the supplier price list for the estimator to check. **Scope:** process review, savings estimate, pricing assistant, staff training, two revision rounds; not included: supplier integrations. **Timeline:** kick-off 18 November, demo 25 November, training 30 November, handover 1 December. **Price:** Quote System GBP 5,900, Quote Assistant GBP 2,400, recommended, with first-year ROI of 516 per cent and payback in under two months, Quote Check GBP 750; 50 per cent deposit, balance on handover. **Next step:** sign and pay the deposit; valid until 1 December 2026. Every phrase traces to the discovery notes, Module 11.9, or the pricing work, Module 11.7, which is exactly what the template is designed to enforce.

### Keeping the template alive

Treat the template as a product. After each proposal, note what the client asked about that the proposal did not answer, which sections they skipped, and which objections arose, Module 11.8. When the same question appears twice, add a line to the template's guidance or a standard paragraph to a section, such as a short explanation of what happens to data. When a section is never read, shorten it. Keep the template under version control with the date of each change, so you can see which version produced which win rate, and resist the temptation to edit old proposals instead of the template, because improvements made to one client's document are lost to the next.

### Formats and delivery

A proposal can be a PDF, a shared document, or a page in a proposal tool; the format matters less than readability on a phone, a clear price table, and an easy way to accept. Many clients read proposals between other tasks, so the first screen should show the problem, the recommended option, and the next step. Long appendices, such as technical architecture or data handling details, can follow the signature block for those who want them. Whatever the format, send the proposal by the date agreed at the end of the discovery call and book the review conversation at the same time, so the client reads it knowing that a short walk-through is already in the diary. That small habit turns many silent proposals into conversations.

### Complexity

Filling a template is linear in its length; choosing sections, word budgets, terms, and phase totals are linear in their inputs.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, filling the template.

```python
import re
from datetime import date, timedelta

TEMPLATE = """Proposal for {client}
PROBLEM: "{problem_quote}". This costs about GBP {annual_cost} a year.
SOLUTION: {outcome}
SCOPE: {deliverables}. Not included: {exclusions}.
TIMELINE: {timeline}
PRICE: {price_options}
NEXT STEP: {next_step}. Valid until {valid_until}."""

def fill(template, values):
    keys = re.findall(r"\{(\w+)\}", template)
    missing = [k for k in dict.fromkeys(keys) if k not in values]
    out = re.sub(r"\{(\w+)\}", lambda m: str(values.get(m.group(1), m.group(0))), template)
    return out, missing

VALUES = {"client": "Harper Joinery", "problem_quote": "Quotes take three hours", "annual_cost": "21,312",
          "outcome": "Same-day quotes.", "deliverables": "pricing assistant, staff training",
          "exclusions": "supplier integrations", "timeline": "kick-off 18 Nov; handover 1 Dec",
          "price_options": "System GBP 5,900 | Assistant GBP 2,400 (recommended) | Check GBP 750",
          "next_step": "Sign and pay the deposit", "valid_until": "1 December 2026"}
text, missing = fill(TEMPLATE, VALUES)
print(missing)
print(text.splitlines()[1])
print(fill(TEMPLATE, {"client": "Harper Joinery"})[1])
```

- **With all ten values, nothing is missing,** and the problem line reads in Priya's words with the cost.
- **With only the client, nine fields are missing,** listed in the order they appear, and left visible in the text so they cannot be sent unnoticed.

Next, choosing sections by size.

```python
BASE = ["problem", "solution", "scope", "timeline", "price", "next step"]

def sections_for(total):
    if total < 3000:
        return BASE
    if total < 15000:
        return BASE[:4] + ["assumptions", "risks"] + BASE[4:]
    return BASE[:3] + ["phases"] + BASE[3:4] + ["assumptions", "risks", "acceptance criteria", "governance"] + BASE[4:]

for total in (2400, 8000, 40000):
    print(total, sections_for(total))
```

- **GBP 2,400: the six core sections.**
- **GBP 8,000: assumptions and risks before the price.**
- **GBP 40,000: phases after scope, and acceptance criteria and governance before the price.**

Then, word budgets.

```python
WORDS = {"problem": 120, "solution": 150, "scope": 200, "timeline": 80, "price": 120, "next step": 40,
         "assumptions": 100, "risks": 120, "phases": 200, "acceptance criteria": 120, "governance": 80}

def word_budget(sections):
    return sum(WORDS[s] for s in sections)

print([word_budget(sections_for(t)) for t in (2400, 8000, 40000)])
```

- **About 700, 930, and 1,330 words.** Even the largest proposal is a short read.

Then, validity and payment terms.

```python
def terms(issue, valid_days, total, deposit_pct):
    valid = (date.fromisoformat(issue) + timedelta(days=valid_days)).isoformat()
    deposit = round(total * deposit_pct, 2)
    return valid, deposit, round(total - deposit, 2)

print(terms("2026-11-17", 14, 2400, 0.5))
print(terms("2026-11-17", 30, 40000, 0.3))
```

- **The Quote Assistant: valid until 1 December, GBP 1,200 deposit, GBP 1,200 balance.**
- **A GBP 40,000 project with a 30 per cent deposit: GBP 12,000 up front, valid for 30 days.**

Finally, phased payments.

```python
def phase_payments(phases):
    out, cum = [], 0.0
    for name, price in phases:
        cum += price
        out.append((name, price, round(cum, 2)))
    return out

for row in phase_payments([("discovery", 4000), ("build", 24000), ("rollout", 12000)]):
    print(row)
```

- **Discovery GBP 4,000, build GBP 24,000, rollout GBP 12,000: GBP 40,000 in total.**
- **The client commits GBP 4,000 first,** and can stop after discovery if the findings change the plan.

**Complexity.** Linear throughout.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Sending unfilled placeholders.** Check the missing list before sending.

**Guessing missing fields.** Go back to the client instead.

**One size for every project.** Add sections as size grows.

**Large proposals without phases.** Start with a paid discovery phase.

**No assumptions.** When they fail, so does the plan.

**No acceptance criteria.** "Done" becomes a debate.

**Long proposals.** Keep to the word budgets.

**No validity date.** Old prices linger.

**Deposits on large projects only at the start.** Use phase payments.

**Template drift.** Improve the template, not each proposal.

**Jargon creeping back.** Re-read in the client's words.

**Copying another client's proposal.** Start from the template, not a previous proposal.
