# Writing Proposals

A proposal is not a sales pitch and not a technical specification. It is a short document that **confirms** what you and the client agreed on the discovery call, Module 11.9, and asks for a decision: here is the problem as you described it, here is what I will do, here is exactly what is and is not included, here is when, here is the price and the return, and here is how to start. A good proposal contains no surprises; the client should recognise their own words in it and find the decision easy. This module covers proposal structure, mirroring the discovery call, follow-up, and handling objections.

Names, figures, and dates are illustrative and continue the Harper Joinery example from Modules 11.3, 11.6, and 11.7.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### The joiner's written quote

After visiting a customer's house, a good joiner sends a written quote that begins with what the customer said: "You want the alcoves either side of the chimney fitted with shelving and cupboards, painted to match the skirting, before your family visit at Christmas." Then the specifics: materials, finish, what is not included (plastering, moving sockets), dates, price, and how to book. The customer reads it and thinks, "Yes, that's exactly what I asked for." Nothing is new; everything is confirmed. That is the effect a proposal should have.

```text
 DISCOVERY CALL (Module 11.9)                 PROPOSAL                          AFTER SENDING
 their words: "quotes take three hours",  ->  1 Problem (their words + cost)    day 2: received? (if unopened)
   "we lose jobs to faster firms",            2 Solution (outcome first)         day 3: offer to walk through it
   "pricing errors"                           3 Scope (deliverables, exclusions) day 7: relevant example
 numbers: GBP 15,024 a year                   4 Timeline (business-day dates)    day 14: expiry, close-out note
 budget, decision-maker, timing               5 Price (options, ROI, payback)
                                              6 Next step (one clear action)    objections: price, timing, trust,
                                                                                  authority, need -> fitting reply
```

### Proposals do not persuade; conversations do

If the client was not persuaded on the call, a proposal will not change their mind. The proposal's job is to make a decision already half-made easy to confirm, and to protect both sides by writing down what was agreed. When a proposal fails, the cause is usually upstream: the discovery call did not establish the problem, the value, the budget, or who decides.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### Structure

A proposal needs six sections, in this order:

1. **Problem.** In the client's words, with the cost you measured together, Module 11.3.
2. **Solution.** The outcome first, then briefly how; no technical jargon the client did not use.
3. **Scope.** Deliverables, revision rounds, and exclusions, Module 11.6.
4. **Timeline.** Milestones with dates, on business days.
5. **Price.** Options, highest first, each with ROI and payback, and the recommended option highlighted, Module 11.7; payment terms.
6. **Next step.** One clear action, such as signing and paying the deposit, with a date the proposal is valid until.

Sections about you, your history, and your tools belong at the end, if at all; one relevant case study, Module 11.5, is enough proof. Keep the whole proposal to a few pages.

### Mirroring the discovery call

Clients trust a proposal that uses their own words. During discovery, write down the exact phrases the client uses for their problem; then use them in the problem and solution sections. A simple measure is the share of noted phrases that appear in the proposal:

$$\text{mirror score} = \frac{|\{\text{phrases appearing in the proposal}\}|}{|\{\text{phrases noted on the call}\}|}$$

A low score means the proposal is written in your language, not theirs. A score of 1 is not the goal in itself; the point is that each important concern is addressed in terms the client recognises.

### Timeline

Dates should be on **business days** and conservative: a milestone date the client remembers is a promise. Starting from a kick-off date, add business days for each milestone in turn, skipping weekends, and add buffers for client feedback, which is usually the slowest part of any project.

### Price and payment terms

Present the options from Module 11.7, highest first, each with its first-year ROI and payback, and the recommendation clearly marked. State payment terms: commonly a deposit before starting, such as 50 per cent, and the balance on delivery, with invoices due within a stated number of days. Deposits filter out uncommitted clients and protect your cash flow.

### Handling objections

Objections usually fall into a few categories, each with a fitting response:

| Category | Sounds like | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Price | "More than I expected", "over budget" | Reduce scope or start with a smaller tier; do not discount the same scope |
| Timing | "Not now", "next quarter" | Agree a start date and a smaller first phase |
| Trust | "Not sure it works", "we tried software before" | Show a case study; offer a paid pilot with a clear success measure |
| Authority | "I need to check with my partner" | Offer a short call with the decision-maker |
| Need | "We manage fine" | Revisit the measured cost of the problem |

Discounting the same scope teaches the client that your prices are negotiable and reduces the value you deliver per hour; changing scope keeps the price honest. If an objection reveals that the problem or value was not established, the honest response is to go back to discovery, not to push harder. Testimonials and case studies used to answer trust objections must be genuine, Module 11.5.

### Follow-up

Silence after a proposal is normal; clients are busy. A simple plan: if the proposal has not been opened after two days, check it arrived; once it is opened, offer to walk through it after about three days; send a relevant example after about a week; and when the validity period ends, send a short, polite close-out note, which often prompts a reply. Stop after that.

### Complexity

Checking structure is linear in sections; the mirror score is linear in phrases times text length; scheduling is linear in business days; classifying objections is linear in patterns.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, checking the structure.

```python
import re
from datetime import date, timedelta

ORDER = ["problem", "solution", "scope", "timeline", "price", "next step"]

def proposal_check(headings):
    h = [x.lower() for x in headings]
    missing = [s for s in ORDER if s not in h]
    present = [s for s in h if s in ORDER]
    out_of_order = present != [s for s in ORDER if s in present]
    return missing, out_of_order

print(proposal_check(["Problem", "Solution", "Scope", "Timeline", "Price", "Next step"]))
print(proposal_check(["About me", "Price", "Solution", "Problem"]))
```

- **The complete proposal has nothing missing and is in order.**
- **The weak draft starts with the writer and the price,** lacks scope, timeline, and a next step, and is out of order.
- **Extra sections, such as "About me", are allowed;** they are simply not checked.

Next, mirroring the client's words.

```python
def mirror_score(phrases, text):
    t = text.lower()
    used = [p for p in phrases if p.lower() in t]
    return round(len(used) / len(phrases), 2), [p for p in phrases if p not in used]

PHRASES = ["quotes take three hours", "we lose jobs to faster firms", "pricing errors"]
DRAFT = "Today quotes take three hours and pricing errors cost about GBP 6,900 a year."
print(mirror_score(PHRASES, DRAFT))
print(mirror_score(PHRASES, "Our AI solution leverages large language models."))
```

- **The draft reuses two of Priya's three phrases;** the missing one, losing jobs to faster firms, is her biggest worry and belongs in the problem section.
- **The jargon version scores zero.** Priya would not recognise her problem in it.

Then, scheduling milestones on business days.

```python
def add_business_days(d, n):
    while n > 0:
        d += timedelta(days=1)
        if d.weekday() < 5:
            n -= 1
    return d

def schedule(start, milestones):
    d = date.fromisoformat(start)
    while d.weekday() >= 5:
        d += timedelta(days=1)
    out = []
    for name, days in milestones:
        d = add_business_days(d, days)
        out.append((name, d.isoformat()))
    return out

PLAN = [("kick-off", 1), ("pricing assistant demo", 5), ("staff training", 3), ("handover", 1)]
print(schedule("2026-11-17", PLAN))
```

- **Kick-off on 18 November, demo on 25 November, training on 30 November, handover on 1 December.**
- **Weekends are skipped,** and a weekend start moves to Monday.
- **Ten business days in total match the package's timeline,** Module 11.6.

Then, classifying objections.

```python
OBJECTIONS = [("price", r"expensive|budget|cost|cheaper|afford"),
              ("timing", r"not now|next (year|quarter|month)|busy|later"),
              ("trust", r"not sure it works|guarantee|proof|risk|tried .*before|didn't work"),
              ("authority", r"(check|talk|speak) with|partner|board|boss"),
              ("need", r"don't need|not a priority|manage fine|fine as is")]
RESPONSES = {"price": "reduce scope or start with a smaller tier; do not discount the same scope",
             "timing": "agree a start date and a smaller first phase",
             "trust": "show a case study and offer a paid pilot with a clear success measure",
             "authority": "offer a short call with the decision-maker",
             "need": "revisit the measured cost of the problem",
             "unclear": "ask a clarifying question"}

def classify_objection(text):
    t = text.lower()
    for cat, pat in OBJECTIONS:
        if re.search(pat, t):
            return cat, RESPONSES[cat]
    return "unclear", RESPONSES["unclear"]

for o in ["It's more expensive than I expected", "We tried software before and it didn't work", "Hmm"]:
    print(classify_objection(o))
```

- **Price objections get a scope change, not a discount.**
- **"We tried software before" is a trust objection:** a case study and a paid pilot answer it.
- **Unclear replies get a question.** Guessing the objection wastes the conversation.

Finally, the follow-up plan.

```python
def follow_up(sent, today, viewed, replied, valid_days=14):
    age = (date.fromisoformat(today) - date.fromisoformat(sent)).days
    if replied:
        return "respond to their reply"
    if age > valid_days:
        return "send a close-out note; the proposal has expired"
    if not viewed and age >= 2:
        return "check they received it"
    if viewed and age >= 7:
        return "second follow-up with a relevant example"
    if viewed and age >= 3:
        return "first follow-up: offer to walk through it"
    return "wait"

for args in [("2026-11-17", "2026-11-19", False, False), ("2026-11-17", "2026-11-20", True, False),
             ("2026-11-17", "2026-12-05", True, False)]:
    print(args[1], follow_up(*args))
```

- **Unopened after two days: check it arrived.**
- **Opened, three days on: offer a walk-through.**
- **Past the 14-day validity: a polite close-out note,** then stop.

**Complexity.** Linear throughout.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Proposals without a discovery call.** They read as guesses. Talk first.

**Starting with yourself.** Start with their problem.

**Technical language.** Use the client's words.

**Missing exclusions.** Disputes follow. List them.

**Optimistic dates.** Client feedback is slow. Add buffers.

**One price only.** Offer options, highest first.

**Discounting at the first objection.** Change scope instead.

**Long proposals.** A few pages; nobody reads forty.

**No validity date.** Proposals linger. State one.

**No deposit.** Uncommitted clients and cash-flow gaps. Ask for one.

**Endless follow-up.** Three touches, then a close-out note.

**Fake proof.** Use genuine case studies and testimonials only.
