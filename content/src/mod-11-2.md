# The 10 Monetization Models

There are only two basic ways to earn money from AI skills: **sell your time** to people who need something built or solved, or **sell things** you have built once and can sell many times. Every model in this module is a variation on those two, and they differ in a few measurable ways: how fast the first money arrives, how much of each sale you keep, how far income can grow without more of your hours, and how much risk you take before anyone pays. This module describes ten models, then gives you the arithmetic to compare them for your own situation.

The ratings and figures in this module are illustrative judgements for teaching, not market data. Module 11.1 covered realistic expectations; the numbers here should be replaced with your own as soon as you have them.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### A joiner's choices

A skilled joiner can earn in several ways. **Taking commissions**: build a custom kitchen for one client at a time, paid well and quickly, but only while working. **Hiring a team**: take bigger jobs and earn on others' work, with management and payroll to carry. **Advising**: tell a manufacturer how to fix its production line, for a high fee per day. **Selling a standard product**: a bookshelf design built to order at a fixed price. **Selling plans**: drawings anyone can buy and build from, made once and sold many times. **Teaching**: a course in joinery. **Building an audience**: a channel or newsletter that earns from sponsors. The same skill, very different businesses.

```text
                     SELL TIME                                  SELL THINGS
  speed to first money: fast ------------------------------------------> slow
  scalability:          low  ------------------------------------------> high
  risk before revenue:  low  ------------------------------------------> high

  freelancing   consulting   retainers   productised   agency  | templates  courses  content  affiliate  micro-SaaS
  (projects)    (advice)     (ongoing)   service       (team)  | (prompts,  (teach)  (audience (referral  (software
                                         (fixed scope)         |  workflows)          + sponsors) fees)     subscription)
```

### Why most people should start on the left

Products need an audience, a proven problem, and months of work before the first sale; services need one client who has a problem today. Services also teach you what people will pay for, which is exactly what a product needs. The usual path, sometimes called stair-stepping, is to start with services, package what repeats, and build products from what you learn.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### The ten models

**Selling time:**

1. **Freelancing.** Projects for clients, priced by the hour or project: build an automation, a chatbot, a RAG system. Fast to start, limited by your hours.
2. **Consulting.** Advice and strategy rather than building: AI readiness reviews, tool selection, process redesign. High rates per hour, but you need credibility.
3. **Retainers and maintenance.** Ongoing monthly fees to run, monitor, and improve what you built. Predictable income; Module 11.12.
4. **Productised service.** A fixed-scope, fixed-price offer delivered the same way each time, such as "a quote assistant for joinery workshops in two weeks". Repeatable work becomes faster and more profitable; Module 11.6.
5. **Agency.** A team delivering services under your brand. Scales beyond your hours, at the cost of hiring, management, and thinner margins per project.

**Selling things:**

6. **Templates and assets.** Prompt packs, automation workflows, Notion systems, starter code. Low cost to make; crowded markets.
7. **Courses and education.** Teaching what you know, live or recorded. High margins once made, but selling them needs an audience.
8. **Content and audience.** A newsletter, channel, or community earning from sponsors, subscriptions, or leads for your services. Slow to build, compounding once built.
9. **Affiliate income.** Commissions for referring customers to tools. Little effort per sale, but income depends on traffic and on programmes you do not control.
10. **Micro-SaaS.** A small software product sold by subscription. The most scalable and the riskiest: it can take many months before it covers its costs, if it ever does.

### Comparing models

Five dimensions capture most of the difference, rated here from 1 to 5: **effort** to start and run, **margin** kept per sale, **scalability** beyond your hours, **speed** to first revenue, and **risk** of spending effort before anyone pays. Effort and risk are bad when high, so a weighted score inverts them:

$$\text{score} = \sum_{k \in \{\text{margin, scalability, speed}\}} w_k\, r_k + \sum_{k \in \{\text{effort, risk}\}} w_k\, (6 - r_k)$$

The weights are yours: someone who needs income next month weights speed heavily; someone with savings and a long horizon weights scalability.

### Unit economics

For any sale, the **contribution** is what you keep:

$$\text{contribution} = p(1 - f) - v, \qquad \text{margin} = \frac{\text{contribution}}{p}$$

with price $p$, a platform or payment fee share $f$, and variable cost $v$ per sale, such as API costs or tools used for that client. The number of sales needed to reach a profit target $T$ with fixed costs $F$ is

$$n = \left\lceil \frac{T + F}{\text{contribution}} \right\rceil$$

A template at GBP 49 with a 10 per cent fee contributes GBP 44.10, so GBP 2,000 a month plus GBP 100 of monthly tool costs needs 48 sales, every month. A GBP 1,500 project with GBP 120 of costs contributes GBP 1,380, so the same target needs two clients.

### Selling time: utilisation

When selling time, not every working hour is billable: finding clients, writing proposals, admin, and learning take a share. With a utilisation $u$, the hours needed for a target are

$$h = \left\lceil \frac{T}{\text{rate} \times u} \right\rceil$$

At GBP 50 an hour and 60 per cent utilisation, GBP 4,000 needs 134 working hours a month.

### Combining models over time

A service income can start at once; a product's income starts later and grows. Modelling each as a starting level plus a monthly growth after a delay shows when the product overtakes the service, the **crossover month**. Until then, the service funds the product. With a service steady at GBP 1,500 a month and a product starting in month 4 and growing by GBP 200 a month, the product passes the service in month 11.

### Complexity

Ranking $m$ models on $k$ dimensions is $O(mk + m \log m)$; the economics are constant time; simulating $n$ months of $s$ streams is $O(ns)$.

First, ranking the models by your own priorities.

```python
import math

MODELS = {
    "freelancing":         {"effort": 3, "margin": 4, "scalability": 1, "speed": 5, "risk": 2},
    "agency":              {"effort": 5, "margin": 3, "scalability": 4, "speed": 3, "risk": 4},
    "consulting":          {"effort": 3, "margin": 5, "scalability": 2, "speed": 3, "risk": 2},
    "productised service": {"effort": 3, "margin": 4, "scalability": 3, "speed": 4, "risk": 2},
    "retainers":           {"effort": 2, "margin": 4, "scalability": 2, "speed": 3, "risk": 2},
    "templates":           {"effort": 2, "margin": 5, "scalability": 4, "speed": 3, "risk": 3},
    "micro-saas":          {"effort": 5, "margin": 4, "scalability": 5, "speed": 1, "risk": 5},
    "courses":             {"effort": 4, "margin": 5, "scalability": 5, "speed": 2, "risk": 4},
    "content":             {"effort": 4, "margin": 4, "scalability": 5, "speed": 1, "risk": 4},
    "affiliate":           {"effort": 2, "margin": 5, "scalability": 4, "speed": 1, "risk": 5},
}

def rank_models(models, weights):
    scores = {}
    for name, m in models.items():
        s = sum(weights[k] * (6 - m[k] if k in ("effort", "risk") else m[k]) for k in weights)
        scores[name] = round(s, 2)
    return sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda kv: (-kv[1], kv[0]))

print(rank_models(MODELS, {"effort": 1, "margin": 1, "scalability": 1, "speed": 1, "risk": 1})[:4])
print(rank_models(MODELS, {"effort": 0, "margin": 1, "scalability": 0, "speed": 3, "risk": 1})[:3])
print(rank_models(MODELS, {"effort": 0, "margin": 1, "scalability": 3, "speed": 0, "risk": 0})[:3])
```

- **Equal weights favour templates, productised services, then consulting and freelancing.** The ratings are illustrative; replace them.
- **Weighting speed, as Sam must, puts freelancing first.**
- **Weighting scalability puts courses, then content and micro-SaaS, on top,** all slow to start.

Next, unit margins.

```python
def margin(price, variable_costs, fee_pct=0.0):
    net = price * (1 - fee_pct) - variable_costs
    return round(net, 2), round(net / price, 3)

print(margin(49, 0, 0.10), margin(1500, 120), margin(29, 3, 0.05))
```

- **A GBP 49 template with a 10 per cent fee keeps GBP 44.10, a 90 per cent margin.**
- **A GBP 1,500 project with GBP 120 of costs keeps GBP 1,380, 92 per cent,** but the margin ignores your hours, which the next functions account for.
- **A GBP 29 subscription with GBP 3 of API costs and a 5 per cent fee keeps GBP 24.55.**

Then, units needed for a target.

```python
def units_needed(target_profit, fixed_costs, price, variable_costs, fee_pct=0.0):
    unit, _ = margin(price, variable_costs, fee_pct)
    if unit <= 0:
        return None
    return math.ceil((target_profit + fixed_costs) / unit)

print(units_needed(2000, 100, 49, 0, 0.10), units_needed(2000, 0, 1500, 120), units_needed(10, 0, 5, 6))
```

- **48 template sales a month versus 2 projects for the same GBP 2,000.** Products need volume, and volume needs an audience.
- **Fixed costs, here GBP 100 of tools, add to the target.**
- **A negative contribution returns None:** selling more only loses more.

Then, hours needed when selling time.

```python
def hours_needed(target, hourly, utilisation):
    return math.ceil(target / hourly / utilisation)

print(hours_needed(4000, 50, 0.6), hours_needed(4000, 50, 0.8))
```

- **134 hours at 60 per cent utilisation; 100 at 80 per cent.** Selling and admin time is real work.
- **Raising utilisation is a lever:** productised services and retainers reduce the time spent finding and scoping work.

Finally, mixing a service with a product over time.

```python
def mix(months, streams):
    totals = []
    for m in range(1, months + 1):
        totals.append(round(sum(s["start"] + s["growth"] * (m - s["delay"]) if m > s["delay"] else 0 for s in streams), 2))
    return totals

def crossover(months, service, product):
    for m in range(1, months + 1):
        if mix(m, [product])[-1] > mix(m, [service])[-1]:
            return m
    return None

SERVICE = {"start": 1500, "growth": 0, "delay": 0}
PRODUCT = {"start": 0, "growth": 200, "delay": 3}
print(mix(12, [SERVICE, PRODUCT]))
print(crossover(24, SERVICE, PRODUCT))
```

- **The service pays GBP 1,500 from month 1; the product adds nothing until month 4, then grows.**
- **Combined income reaches GBP 3,300 in month 12.**
- **The product overtakes the service in month 11.** Until then the service pays the bills, which is the point of starting with it.

**Complexity.** Linear in models and months.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Starting with a product.** No audience, no sales. Start with services and learn the problem.

**Choosing by the most exciting model.** Weight by what you need: speed, risk, or scale.

**Ignoring fees and costs.** Platform and payment fees and API costs eat margins. Use contribution, not price.

**Forgetting unbilled time.** Utilisation of 50 to 70 per cent is common. Plan for it.

**Agency too early.** Managing others before you can sell reliably multiplies problems. Productise first.

**Templates in crowded markets.** Many sell few copies. Distribution matters more than the template.

**Courses without an audience.** Build the audience, or sell to your clients' teams.

**Affiliate dependence.** Programmes change terms. Treat it as a bonus, not a plan.

**Micro-SaaS underestimates.** Support, billing, security, and marketing take most of the time. Budget months.

**Mixing too many models.** Focus beats breadth early. Add one model at a time.

**Never revisiting.** Your ratings change as you learn. Re-rank quarterly.

**Copying someone else's numbers.** Online income claims are not your plan; Module 11.1.
