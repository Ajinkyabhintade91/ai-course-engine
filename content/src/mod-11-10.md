# Client Onboarding

The moment a client says yes is when many freelancers relax, and when many projects start to go wrong. The weeks between acceptance and kick-off set the tone: whether the agreement is written down, whether money arrives on time, whether you get the access you need without creating security risks, and whether the client knows when they will hear from you. A short, repeatable onboarding process prevents most of the disputes, delays, and late payments that hurt small businesses. This module covers contracts, invoices, and kick-off; access, credentials, and security hygiene; and setting communication cadence and expectations.

UK rules quoted here are from GOV.UK and the National Cyber Security Centre at the time of writing (November 2026). This module is general guidance, not legal or tax advice; use a contract reviewed by a professional for your circumstances.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### Before the first cut

A joiner who has won a job does not turn up the next morning and start cutting. They confirm the order in writing, take a deposit, arrange access to the house and where to park, find out where the fuse box is and who has the keys, agree how and when they will update the customer, and only then start. Skipping these steps does not save time; it moves the time to later, when it is spent on arguments about what was agreed, chasing payment, or waiting for someone to open the door.

```text
 YES ---> CONTRACT ---> DEPOSIT INVOICE ---> ACCESS ---> KICK-OFF ---> WEEKLY UPDATES ---> FINAL INVOICE
          scope, price,  50 per cent,        least       success       same day each     balance, 14-day
          IP, data,      unique number,      privilege,  measure,      week; response    terms; statutory
          changes,       14-day terms        own account named         time agreed       interest if late
          liability,                         MFA, expiry contacts
          termination
 checklist: contract signed | deposit paid | access granted | kick-off booked | success measure | contacts named
```

### Onboarding is part of the product

Clients judge you by how the start feels. A clear welcome, a signed agreement, a professional invoice, careful handling of their systems, and a predictable rhythm of updates tell them their decision was right, before any code is written. That confidence makes them easier to work with and more likely to refer you.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### Contracts

A short written contract, often a statement of work under general terms, should cover:

- **Scope and deliverables,** copied from the package and proposal, Modules 11.6 and 11.8.
- **Price and payment terms:** deposit, balance, due dates.
- **Change requests:** how out-of-scope work is quoted and approved.
- **Intellectual property:** who owns the delivered code, prompts, and workflows, and when ownership transfers, commonly on full payment; what you keep, such as reusable components.
- **Confidentiality.**
- **Data protection:** if you handle personal data on the client's behalf, you are likely a **processor** under the GDPR, and Article 28 requires a written contract with specific terms.
- **Liability cap:** limiting your liability, commonly to the fees paid.
- **Termination:** how either side can end the agreement and what is paid for work done.

Missing clauses are where disputes start. A contract checklist catches them before signing.

### Invoices

In the UK, GOV.UK lists what an invoice must include: a unique identification number; your name or company name, address, and contact information; the customer's name and address; a clear description of what you are charging for; the supply date and the invoice date; the amounts; VAT if applicable; and the total owed. Sole traders must also include their name and any business name used. You must register for VAT when taxable turnover exceeds GBP 90,000, the threshold on GOV.UK at the time of writing; below it, most freelancers do not charge VAT. If registered, VAT at the standard 20 per cent is added:

$$\text{total} = \text{subtotal} \times (1 + v), \qquad \text{balance} = \text{total} - \text{paid}$$

with $v = 0.2$ when registered and 0 otherwise. A **deposit** invoice, often 50 per cent, is raised on signing; the **final** invoice lists the full work, any agreed extras, and the deposit already paid.

### Late payment

UK law gives businesses rights when other businesses pay late. If no payment date is agreed, payment is late 30 days after the invoice or delivery, whichever is later; agreed terms for business transactions should usually be within 60 days. You can then claim **statutory interest** of 8 per cent plus the Bank of England base rate, unless the contract sets a different rate, plus a **fixed sum** for recovery costs: GBP 40 on debts up to GBP 999.99, GBP 70 from GBP 1,000 to GBP 9,999.99, and GBP 100 for GBP 10,000 or more:

$$\text{interest} = A \times (0.08 + r_{\text{base}}) \times \frac{d}{365}$$

for an amount $A$ late by $d$ days. Few freelancers use these rights, but stating them in your terms, and knowing the figures, makes polite reminders firmer.

### Access, credentials, and security hygiene

AI projects need access to client systems: documents, email, spreadsheets, accounting packages, APIs. Each access grant is a security risk for the client and a liability for you. Good practice, consistent with the National Cyber Security Centre's small business guidance:

- **Your own account,** never a shared login, so actions are attributable and access can be removed.
- **Least privilege:** read-only if you only need to read; admin only if the work truly requires it.
- **Multi-factor authentication** on every account.
- **Secrets through a password manager or the system's own sharing,** never by email or chat.
- **An expiry or review date,** with access removed at handover, Module 11.11.
- **API keys** scoped to the task, stored outside code, and rotated at the end, Module 8.14.

Write the access plan into the onboarding checklist and confirm removal at the end.

### Kick-off

Do not start work until the basics are in place: contract signed, deposit paid, access granted, kick-off call booked, the **success measure** agreed, such as median pricing time per quote of ten minutes or less, and named contacts on both sides. The kick-off call confirms goals, contacts, the schedule, and how communication will work.

### Communication cadence and expectations

Agree how and when you will communicate: a weekly written update on a fixed day, what it contains (done, next, blockers, decisions needed), your normal response time to messages, and the channel. Predictable updates prevent the anxious "how is it going?" messages that interrupt work, and they create a written record of decisions.

### Complexity

Checking a contract is linear in clauses; invoices are linear in lines; access reviews are linear in requests; update schedules are linear in weeks.

Next, raising invoices.

```python
def invoice(lines, vat_registered, issue, terms_days=14, vat_rate=0.20, paid=0.0):
    subtotal = round(sum(q * p for _, q, p in lines), 2)
    vat = round(subtotal * vat_rate, 2) if vat_registered else 0.0
    total = round(subtotal + vat, 2)
    due = (date.fromisoformat(issue) + timedelta(days=terms_days)).isoformat()
    return {"subtotal": subtotal, "vat": vat, "total": total, "balance": round(total - paid, 2), "due": due}

print(invoice(DEPOSIT, False, "2026-11-17"))

print(invoice(FINAL, False, "2026-12-01", paid=1200))
```

- **The deposit invoice is GBP 1,200, due on 1 December** with 14-day terms; Sam is not VAT-registered.
- **If Sam were registered, VAT of GBP 240 would be added.**
- **The final invoice lists the full GBP 2,400, two agreed extra sessions, and the deposit paid,** leaving a balance of GBP 1,500.

Then, late payment.

```python
def late_payment(amount, days_late, base_rate):
    interest = round(amount * (0.08 + base_rate) * days_late / 365, 2)
    fixed = 40 if amount < 1000 else (70 if amount < 10000 else 100)
    return interest, fixed, round(interest + fixed, 2)

print(late_payment(1200, 30, 0.04))
print(late_payment(12000, 60, 0.04))
```

- **GBP 1,200 paid 30 days late: GBP 11.84 of interest plus a GBP 70 fixed sum.** The base rate here is illustrative; use the current one.
- **The fixed sum depends on the debt's size:** GBP 40, 70, or 100.
- **Usually a reminder citing these rights is enough;** claiming them is a decision about the relationship.

Then, reviewing access.

```python
def access_issues(reqs):
    out = []
    for r in reqs:
        probs = []
        if r.get("shared_login"):
            probs.append("use your own account, not a shared login")
        if r.get("level") == "admin" and r.get("needs") != "admin":
            probs.append("ask for least privilege")
        if not r.get("expires"):
            probs.append("set an expiry or review date")
        if r.get("via") == "email":
            probs.append("share secrets through a password manager, not email")
        if not r.get("mfa"):
            probs.append("turn on multi-factor authentication")
        out.append((r["system"], probs))
    return out

REQUESTS = [{"system": "supplier price list (shared drive)", "level": "viewer", "needs": "viewer",
             "expires": "2027-01-31", "via": "invite", "mfa": True},
            {"system": "accounts package", "level": "admin", "needs": "read", "shared_login": True,
             "via": "email", "mfa": False}]
for system, probs in access_issues(REQUESTS):
    print(system, probs)
```

- **The price list access is right:** viewer, own invitation, expiry date, MFA.
- **The accounts package fails all five checks:** a shared admin login sent by email without MFA or expiry. Sam should decline it and ask for a read-only account.

Finally, kick-off readiness and update dates.

```python
KICKOFF = ["contract signed", "deposit paid", "access granted", "kick-off call booked",
           "success measure agreed", "contacts named"]

def kickoff_ready(done):
    missing = [k for k in KICKOFF if k not in done]
    return (not missing), missing

def update_dates(start, weeks, weekday=4):
    d = date.fromisoformat(start)
    while d.weekday() != weekday:
        d += timedelta(days=1)
    return [(d + timedelta(weeks=i)).isoformat() for i in range(weeks)]

print(kickoff_ready(["contract signed", "deposit paid", "access granted"]))
print(update_dates("2026-11-17", 3))
```

- **Three items are still open,** so work does not start yet.
- **Updates every Friday: 20 November, 27 November, 4 December.**
- **Weekday 4 is Friday** in Python's numbering, Monday being 0.

**Complexity.** Linear throughout.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Starting without a signed contract.** Disputes have nothing to refer to.

**No deposit.** Cash-flow risk and uncommitted clients.

**Vague intellectual property terms.** Agree who owns what, and when.

**Ignoring data protection.** Handling customers' personal data usually makes you a processor.

**Accepting shared admin logins.** Ask for your own least-privilege account.

**Passwords by email.** Use a password manager.

**Access left open after handover.** Remove it and confirm in writing.

**Invoices missing required details.** Use the GOV.UK list.

**Silent late payments.** Remind politely, citing your terms.

**No agreed success measure.** "Done" becomes a matter of opinion.

**Irregular updates.** Clients worry and interrupt. Pick a day.

**Over-promising response times.** Promise what you can keep.
