# Creating Service Packages

Custom projects are a good way to start, Module 11.2, but each one needs its own discovery, scoping, proposal, and pricing, and each one is a little different to deliver. Once you have done similar work three or four times, you can turn it into a **productised service**: a named package with a fixed scope, fixed deliverables, a fixed timeline, and a fixed price, delivered the same way every time. Packages are easier for clients to buy, easier for you to sell, and more profitable to deliver. This module covers tiered packages, defining scope, deliverables, and exclusions, and naming and positioning an offer.

Prices and figures in the examples are illustrative and in pounds sterling.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### From commissions to a catalogue

A joiner who builds the same type of fitted wardrobe for the fifth time stops quoting from scratch. They publish a catalogue: three sizes, each with listed features, a lead time, and a price, and a clear note of what is not included, such as moving radiators or plastering. Customers choose quickly, the joiner builds faster because the method is fixed, and arguments about "I thought that was included" largely disappear. Packaging AI services works the same way.

```text
 CUSTOM PROJECT                                  PACKAGED SERVICE
 discovery -> scoping -> bespoke proposal        choose a tier -> confirm fit -> start date
 price negotiated each time                      price published, value explained
 deliverables vary                               deliverables listed, exclusions listed
 delivery reinvented                             delivery follows a checklist

            Quote Check            Quote Assistant          Quote System
 price      GBP 750                GBP 2,400                GBP 5,900
 days       3                      10                       25
 includes   review, savings est.   + pricing assistant,     + supplier price sync,
                                     staff training           follow-up automation
 excludes   building software      supplier integrations    (anything not listed)
 ladder:    each tier includes the one below; price steps 3.2x and 2.5x
```

### Why packages raise income

Packaging cuts the unbilled hours spent on sales and scoping, which Module 11.1 showed dominate early work; it lets you price by value rather than hours, Module 11.7; and repetition makes delivery faster, so the effective hourly rate rises with each delivery.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### Productised services

A productised service has five fixed elements: **scope**, the problem it addresses; **deliverables**, what the client receives; **timeline**; **price**; and **process**, the steps you follow. It typically emerges from repeated custom work: you notice the same problem, the same solution, and the same questions each time. Brian Casel, who popularised the term, describes it as turning a service into something closer to a product, sold the same way to many clients.

### Tiered packages

Offering three tiers, often called good, better, best, lets clients choose their level of commitment instead of a yes or no. A useful ladder has these properties:

- **Each tier includes the one below**, so moving up is clearly more.
- **Prices rise by meaningful steps**, roughly 1.5 to 3.5 times, so the tiers feel distinct without the top looking absurd:

$$1.5 \le \frac{p_{k+1}}{p_k} \le 3.5$$

- **The middle tier is the one you expect most clients to buy.** Research on choice behaviour describes a tendency to avoid extremes and to be influenced by a less attractive third option, the decoy effect; a clear top tier makes the middle look reasonable.
- **The bottom tier is a low-risk entry**, often a diagnostic such as a process review, which can lead into the others.

### Scope, deliverables, and exclusions

Every package needs a written list of **deliverables**, a stated number of **revision rounds**, a **timeline**, and **exclusions**: things clients commonly assume are included but are not. Exclusions prevent most disputes. In AI work, typical exclusions are integrations with other systems, ongoing API costs, data cleaning beyond a stated amount, and support after a handover period, which a retainer can cover, Module 11.12.

**Scope creep**, the gradual expansion of work beyond what was agreed, is the main threat to fixed-price profitability. A package makes it visible: any request is either a listed deliverable, a listed exclusion, or something not mentioned. The last two are **change requests**, priced separately or added in a higher tier, Module 11.11.

### Naming and positioning

A package name should say what the client gets, not what technology it uses: "Quote Assistant" rather than "LLM Integration Package". A **positioning line** states who it is for, the outcome, and how fast:

> [Offer] helps [who] [outcome] in [timeframe].

"Quote Assistant helps joinery workshops quote the same day in two weeks." Keep it under about 25 words and free of buzzwords such as "solutions" or "AI-powered".

### Capacity

Packages also make capacity planning simple. With $H$ working hours a month available for delivery and a package taking $d$ days of $h$ hours, the number you can deliver per month is

$$n = \left\lfloor \frac{H}{d \times h} \right\rfloor$$

A package that does not fit in one month's capacity must span months, which affects cash flow and how many you can sell.

### From custom work to a package

The raw material for a package is your record of past projects. For each similar project, list the problem, what you delivered, how long each part took, which questions the client asked, and what they asked for afterwards. Items that appear in every project become **deliverables**; items that appear in some become the difference between tiers; requests that kept appearing but that you did not want to do become **exclusions**. The time records give the timeline and, with Module 11.7, the price. Write the delivery steps as a checklist, including the onboarding questions, Module 11.10, so each delivery starts the same way.

Test a new package on one or two clients at an introductory price before publishing it widely, and ask them what was unclear. Expect to revise the deliverables and exclusions after the first few deliveries; a package is a living document, not a contract template frozen on day one.

### Presenting packages

A package page usually has the positioning line at the top, a short description of the problem in the client's words, the three tiers side by side with the middle one highlighted, a "how it works" section with the delivery steps and timeline, the exclusions in plain language, one or two case studies, Module 11.5, and a single next step, such as booking a short call. Prices can be shown or summarised as "from" figures; showing them filters out clients who cannot afford them and saves both sides time.

### Complexity

Validating a package is linear in its fields; checking a ladder is linear in tiers times deliverables; classifying requests is linear in requests; capacity is linear in tiers.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, validating packages.

```python
import math

TIERS = [
    {"name": "Quote Check", "price": 750, "days": 3, "deliverables": ["process review", "savings estimate"],
     "revisions": 1, "exclusions": ["building software"]},
    {"name": "Quote Assistant", "price": 2400, "days": 10,
     "deliverables": ["process review", "savings estimate", "pricing assistant", "staff training"],
     "revisions": 2, "exclusions": ["supplier integrations"]},
    {"name": "Quote System", "price": 5900, "days": 25,
     "deliverables": ["process review", "savings estimate", "pricing assistant", "staff training",
                      "supplier price sync", "follow-up automation"], "revisions": 3, "exclusions": []},
]
REQUIRED = ["name", "price", "days", "deliverables", "revisions", "exclusions"]

def package_issues(p):
    issues = [f"missing {k}" for k in REQUIRED if k not in p]
    if "deliverables" in p and not p["deliverables"]:
        issues.append("no deliverables")
    if "exclusions" in p and not p["exclusions"] and p.get("price", 0) < 5000:
        issues.append("state at least one exclusion")
    if "revisions" in p and p["revisions"] > 3:
        issues.append("too many revisions included")
    return issues

print([package_issues(t) for t in TIERS])
print(package_issues({"name": "X", "price": 500, "deliverables": [], "revisions": 5, "exclusions": []}))
```

- **All three tiers are complete.**
- **A careless package fails four checks:** no timeline, no deliverables, no exclusions, and unlimited-looking revisions.
- **The top tier may have no exclusions,** since it includes everything Sam offers; cheaper tiers must say what they leave out.

Next, checking the ladder.

```python
def ladder_issues(tiers):
    issues = []
    for a, b in zip(tiers, tiers[1:]):
        if b["price"] <= a["price"]:
            issues.append(f"{b['name']} is not priced above {a['name']}")
        else:
            r = b["price"] / a["price"]
            if r < 1.5 or r > 3.5:
                issues.append(f"{a['name']} to {b['name']} price step {r:.2f}x outside 1.5x to 3.5x")
        if not set(a["deliverables"]) <= set(b["deliverables"]):
            issues.append(f"{b['name']} drops deliverables from {a['name']}")
    return issues

print(ladder_issues(TIERS))
print(ladder_issues([TIERS[0], {**TIERS[1], "price": 4000, "deliverables": ["pricing assistant"]}]))
```

- **Sam's ladder passes: steps of 3.2 and about 2.5 times, each tier containing the last.**
- **A middle tier at GBP 4,000 that drops the review breaks two rules:** a 5.33 times jump and a tier that is not clearly "more".

Then, classifying client requests.

```python
def classify_requests(requests, package):
    out = []
    for r in requests:
        if r in package["deliverables"]:
            out.append((r, "included"))
        elif r in package["exclusions"]:
            out.append((r, "excluded: change request"))
        else:
            out.append((r, "not in scope: change request"))
    return out

print(classify_requests(["staff training", "supplier integrations", "website redesign"], TIERS[1]))
```

- **Training is included; supplier integrations are explicitly excluded; a website redesign was never in scope.**
- **Both of the last two are change requests:** priced separately, or handled by moving up a tier.
- **Having the lists in writing makes this conversation calm,** not personal.

Then, a positioning line.

```python
def positioning(who, outcome, timeframe, offer):
    s = f"{offer} helps {who} {outcome} in {timeframe}."
    issues = []
    if len(s.split()) > 25:
        issues.append("too long")
    if any(w in s.lower() for w in ("solutions", "synergy", "cutting-edge", "ai-powered")):
        issues.append("buzzword")
    return s, issues

print(positioning("joinery workshops", "quote the same day", "two weeks", "Quote Assistant"))
print(positioning("businesses", "unlock synergy with cutting-edge AI-powered solutions", "record time", "Our platform"))
```

- **"Quote Assistant helps joinery workshops quote the same day in two weeks."** Who, outcome, timeframe.
- **The buzzword version says nothing a joiner would act on.**

Finally, capacity.

```python
def capacity(tiers, hours_per_month, hours_per_day=6):
    out = {}
    for t in tiers:
        n = math.floor(hours_per_month / (t["days"] * hours_per_day))
        out[t["name"]] = (n, n * t["price"])
    return out

print(capacity(TIERS, 120))
```

- **With 120 delivery hours a month, Sam can run six Quote Checks or two Quote Assistants.**
- **The Quote System needs 150 hours,** so it spans two months; Sam should sell at most one at a time.
- **Two Quote Assistants earn GBP 4,800,** more than six Quote Checks at GBP 4,500, with fewer clients to win.

**Complexity.** Linear throughout.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Packaging too early.** Package after repeating similar work, not before.

**Vague deliverables.** "AI automation" cannot be checked. List concrete items.

**No exclusions.** Disputes follow. Write them down.

**Unlimited revisions.** They turn fixed prices into open-ended work. State a number.

**Tiers that are not supersets.** Clients get confused. Each tier includes the one below.

**Price steps too small or too large.** Aim for roughly 1.5 to 3.5 times.

**Technical names.** Name the outcome, not the model.

**Buzzword positioning.** Who, outcome, timeframe.

**Ignoring capacity.** Selling more than you can deliver damages reputation.

**Never revisiting prices.** Raise them as delivery gets faster and results accumulate.

**Saying yes to every change.** Price change requests, or offer the next tier.

**Hiding the process.** A short "how it works" section builds trust.
