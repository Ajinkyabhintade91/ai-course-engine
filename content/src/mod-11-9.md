# Discovery Calls

The discovery call is where most deals are won or lost. In thirty minutes you need to understand how the client's process works, where it hurts, what that costs, what they want instead, and whether they can and will buy: their budget, who decides, and when. Done well, the call gives you everything the proposal needs, Module 11.8, in the client's own words, and the client finishes it feeling understood. Done badly, as a pitch with questions sprinkled in, it produces a proposal that guesses and a client who is not sure why they should pay. This module covers call structure and question frameworks, qualifying with budget, authority, need, and timing, taking notes, and closing with clear next steps.

The call with Priya, the figures, and the time splits are illustrative, continuing the Harper Joinery example.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### The joiner's site visit

Before quoting for fitted alcoves, a good joiner visits the house. They do not arrive with a brochure and talk about their tools. They ask how the family uses the room, measure, look at the walls and floors, ask what has annoyed the customer about previous work, what the budget is, whether the partner needs to agree, and when it has to be finished. They listen, write things down, and leave having agreed what happens next: "I'll send a quote by Friday, and we'll talk it through on Monday." The visit is mostly listening. So is a discovery call.

```text
 0     3            11            17        22             27       30 minutes
 |rapport|  current process | impact & cost | outcome | budget/decision/timing | next steps |
          "walk me through"   "how many hours"  "in 3 months"  "who else decides"   proposal date + review date

 listen ~70 per cent, talk ~30 per cent     notes: pains, numbers, exact quotes
 qualify: Budget  Authority  Need  Timing  ->  propose | clarify | not now
```

### Diagnosis before prescription

A doctor who prescribes before examining is negligent; a consultant who proposes before diagnosing is guessing. The discovery call is the examination. It also tests fit in both directions: some prospects do not have a problem worth solving, cannot pay, or cannot decide, and learning that in thirty minutes saves both sides weeks.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### Call structure

A 30-minute call can follow a fixed agenda:

| Stage | Minutes | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Rapport and purpose | 3 | Agree the aim and the time; ask permission to ask questions |
| Current process | 8 | How the work happens today, step by step |
| Impact and cost | 6 | Where it hurts, how often, what it costs in time and money |
| Desired outcome | 5 | What "fixed" looks like and how it would be measured |
| Budget, decision, timing | 5 | Whether and how they can buy |
| Next steps | 3 | A dated, agreed next action |

The agenda must fit the time; a call that overruns signals poor planning, and the most important stage, next steps, is the one that gets cut. Demos and solutions belong in a later meeting, if at all.

### Question frameworks

Good discovery questions ask about **past behaviour and specifics**, not opinions and hypotheticals. Rob Fitzpatrick's book *The Mom Test* makes the case: "would you use this?" invites polite lies; "when did this last happen, and what did you do?" reveals reality. A progression that works:

- **Situation:** "Walk me through how a quote happens today." "Who is involved?"
- **Problem:** "Where does it get stuck?" "What goes wrong most often?"
- **Impact:** "How many hours a week does that take?" "What happens when a quote is late?"
- **Outcome:** "If this were fixed, what would be different in three months?"
- **Qualification:** budget, decision-makers, timing.

Impact questions produce the numbers Module 11.3 needs: hours, volumes, error costs. Ask for examples and estimates, and confirm them later with records.

### Qualifying: BANT and alternatives

**BANT**, budget, authority, need, and timing, is a qualification framework widely attributed to IBM. Each element can be assessed from the notes:

- **Budget:** is there money set aside at or above your minimum price? Unknown is common and should be asked.
- **Authority:** is the person on the call the decision-maker, or are others involved?
- **Need:** is the problem's cost large relative to the price? A simple rule: the annual cost should be at least three times the minimum price, so the client can see a clear return.
- **Timing:** do they want it within, say, three months?

$$\text{need is strong} \iff \text{annual cost} \ge 3 \times \text{minimum price}$$

The verdict follows: if all four are clear, **propose**; if the budget is below your minimum or the need is weak, **not now**, or offer a smaller scope; otherwise **clarify** the open points first, for example with a call including the decision-maker. Other frameworks exist: **MEDDIC**, which adds metrics, decision criteria, decision process, and a champion, suits larger organisations; **CHAMP** puts the client's challenges first. For small-business work, BANT with a strong focus on need is usually enough.

### Taking notes

Notes serve the proposal. Capture three kinds of content:

- **Pains:** sentences describing problems, delays, errors, frustration.
- **Numbers:** hours, volumes, costs, error rates.
- **Exact quotes:** the client's own phrases, in quotation marks, for mirroring, Module 11.8.

Write them during the call, then spend ten minutes afterwards tidying them while memory is fresh. Recording the call requires the client's consent; ask first and respect a refusal.

### Talk ratio

Listening is the point. The share of the call you spend talking is a useful check:

$$\text{talk ratio} = \frac{\text{your speaking time}}{\text{total speaking time}}$$

Above about 0.4, you are probably pitching rather than discovering. Silence after a question is useful; let the client fill it.

### Closing with next steps

Every call ends with an agreed, dated next step that matches the verdict: for a qualified opportunity, a date to send the proposal and a date to review it together; for an unclear one, a follow-up including the missing decision-maker or a summary confirming open questions; for a poor fit, thanks, something useful, and an agreed time to check back. "I'll send something over" without a date is not a next step.

### Complexity

Planning the agenda is linear in stages; qualification is constant time; extracting notes is linear in their length; the talk ratio is linear in speaking segments.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, planning the agenda.

```python
import re

AGENDA = [("rapport and purpose", 3), ("current process", 8), ("impact and cost", 6),
          ("desired outcome", 5), ("budget, decision, timing", 5), ("next steps", 3)]

def agenda_plan(agenda, start_minute=0, length=30):
    t, out = start_minute, []
    for name, mins in agenda:
        out.append((name, t, t + mins))
        t += mins
    return out, t - start_minute <= length

plan, fits = agenda_plan(AGENDA)
print(plan, fits)
print(agenda_plan(AGENDA + [("demo", 10)])[1])
```

- **Six stages fill exactly 30 minutes;** next steps start at minute 27.
- **Adding a 10-minute demo breaks the plan.** Demos belong in another meeting.

Then, qualifying with BANT.

```python
def qualify(notes):
    mp = notes.get("min_price", 0)
    b = notes.get("budget")
    budget = "unknown" if b is None else ("yes" if b >= mp else "no")
    if notes.get("decision_maker_on_call"):
        authority = "yes"
    elif notes.get("other_deciders"):
        authority = "partial"
    else:
        authority = "unknown"
    need = "yes" if notes.get("annual_cost", 0) >= 3 * mp else "weak"
    s = notes.get("start_within_months")
    timing = "unknown" if s is None else ("yes" if s <= 3 else "later")
    result = {"budget": budget, "authority": authority, "need": need, "timing": timing}
    if all(v == "yes" for v in result.values()):
        verdict = "propose"
    elif budget == "no" or need == "weak":
        verdict = "not now"
    else:
        verdict = "clarify"
    return result, verdict

PRIYA = {"budget": 3000, "min_price": 2400, "decision_maker_on_call": True, "annual_cost": 21312, "start_within_months": 1}
print(qualify(PRIYA))
print(qualify({**PRIYA, "decision_maker_on_call": False, "other_deciders": ["business partner"]}))
print(qualify({"min_price": 2400, "annual_cost": 3000}))
```

- **Priya qualifies on all four: propose.** Pricing costs about GBP 21,312 a year, nearly nine times the minimum price.
- **If her partner must decide, the verdict is clarify:** book a call with both.
- **A small problem with nothing else known is "not now":** need is weak, so a proposal would struggle to show a return.

Then, extracting notes for the proposal.

```python
PAIN = r"\b(stuck|slow|lose|losing|error|errors|frustrat|nightmare|hours|late|miss)\w*"
NUM = r"\b\d[\d,.]*\b"

def extract(notes_text):
    lines = [l.strip() for l in notes_text.splitlines() if l.strip()]
    return {"pains": [l for l in lines if re.search(PAIN, l, re.I)],
            "numbers": [l for l in lines if re.search(NUM, l)],
            "quotes": re.findall(r'"([^"]+)"', notes_text)}

NOTES = ('Priya: quoting is a "nightmare"\nEstimator spends 10 hours a week pricing materials\n'
         '"We lose jobs to faster firms"\nUses a spreadsheet\nWants quotes same day')
print(extract(NOTES))
```

- **Three pain lines, one line with a number, two exact quotes.**
- **The quotes go straight into the proposal's problem section;** the number feeds the cost estimate.
- **"Uses a spreadsheet" is context, not pain;** it matters for the solution, not the case.

Finally, talk ratio and closing.

```python
def talk_ratio(segments):
    you = sum(s for who, s in segments if who == "you")
    total = sum(s for _, s in segments)
    r = round(you / total, 2)
    return r, ("talking too much" if r > 0.4 else "good balance")

def close_call(verdict, decider_missing=False):
    if verdict == "propose":
        return "agree a date to send the proposal and a date to review it together"
    if verdict == "clarify":
        return "book a follow-up with the decision-maker" if decider_missing else "send a short summary and confirm the open questions"
    return "thank them, share a useful resource, and agree when to check back"

print(talk_ratio([("you", 120), ("client", 600), ("you", 60), ("client", 420)]))
print(talk_ratio([("you", 900), ("client", 300)]))
print(close_call("propose"))
```

- **Speaking 180 of 1,200 seconds, a ratio of 0.15, is good listening.**
- **0.75 means Sam pitched instead of discovering.**
- **Each verdict has a dated next step;** for Priya, a proposal date and a review date.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Pitching on the call.** Save solutions for the proposal.

**Hypothetical questions.** "Would you use this?" gets polite lies. Ask about the last time.

**No numbers.** Without hours and costs, value pricing is impossible. Ask for estimates.

**Skipping budget.** It feels awkward; it saves weeks. Ask directly.

**Assuming the caller decides.** Ask who else is involved.

**Overrunning.** Next steps get cut. Keep time.

**Talking too much.** Aim for under 40 per cent.

**Recording without consent.** Ask first.

**No dated next step.** Agree dates before hanging up.

**Proposing to poor fits.** Weak need or no budget means not now, or a smaller scope.

**Not writing up immediately.** Details fade within hours.

**Forgetting the client's words.** Write exact phrases in quotation marks.
