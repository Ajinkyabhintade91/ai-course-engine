# How the roadmap works

The previous eleven units covered a great deal: how AI works, prompting, tools, content, automation, agents, coding, local models, and earning a living. Knowledge spread across that much material fades quickly unless it is used. This unit turns the course into a **30-day roadmap**: a daily routine of a little learning, a lot of building, and short reviews, organised into weekly projects that end in a portfolio, an offer, and outreach. This first module explains how the roadmap works: the principles behind it, how to budget and track time, and how to adapt it to your starting level. Module 12.2 gives the plan day by day, and Module 12.3 covers what comes after.

The principles draw on well-established findings in learning research, summarised with references at the end. Hours and splits in the examples are illustrative; set your own.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### An apprenticeship, not a lecture series

Joinery is not learned by watching videos for thirty days and then building a staircase. Apprentices do a little every day: learn a joint in the morning, cut it in the afternoon, have the master check it, and cut it again tomorrow a little better. Each week ends with something finished, a box, a stool, a cabinet, which is better than the last. The roadmap works the same way: short daily learning, daily building, frequent checking, and a finished project every week.

```text
 EACH DAY (about 70 minutes at 7 hours a week, 6 days)
   learn  ~40 per cent   one concept from the course, with its video
   build  ~45 per cent   apply it to this week's project
   review ~15 per cent   recall questions, notes, what went wrong

 EACH WEEK
   project with a deliverable  ->  weekly review: completed %, carry-over, adjust load
 WEEK 1 foundations and prompting | WEEKS 2-3 tools, automation, agents, coding | WEEK 4 portfolio, offer, outreach

 START LEVEL: self-assess six areas 0-5 -> skip what you know (4+), focus on gaps (0-1)
 TRACK: minutes per activity, adherence to plan, practice streak
```

### Why "a little every day" beats "a lot at weekends"

The same total hours spread across many days are remembered far better than the same hours crammed together; and practising recall, answering questions without looking, strengthens memory more than rereading. Daily practice also builds a habit, which matters more over 30 days than any single heroic session.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### Principle 1: daily practice, spaced

The **spacing effect** is one of the most replicated findings in learning research: information studied in several sessions spread over time is retained better than the same amount of study in one block. The roadmap therefore revisits each topic: a concept introduced on day 3 reappears when it is used in the week's project and again in the weekly review.

### Principle 2: projects, not just reading

**Deliberate practice**, the term associated with Anders Ericsson's research on expert performance, describes practice that is focused on specific weaknesses, slightly beyond current ability, and accompanied by feedback. Projects provide all three: a real deliverable exposes what you cannot yet do, stretches you, and gives feedback, because the automation either works or does not. That is why roughly half of each day goes to building.

### Principle 3: review by recall

The **testing effect**, also called retrieval practice, shows that trying to recall information, for example by answering questions, strengthens memory more than restudying it. The Active Recall questions at the end of every module are designed for this; the daily review answers a few of them without looking, then checks.

### Time budgeting

A budget makes the plan realistic. With $W$ hours a week over $d$ days, and shares $s_k$ for each activity:

$$\text{minutes}_k = \frac{60\,W}{d} \times s_k$$

Seven hours over six days is 70 minutes a day: about 28 minutes learning, 32 building, and 10 reviewing. Ten hours over five days is two hours a day. Fixed **timeboxes** help: **Parkinson's law**, the observation that work expands to fill the time available, suggests that a defined 30-minute build session produces more than an open-ended evening.

### Tracking

A simple log, date, activity, minutes, takes a few seconds a day and answers three questions at the weekly review: how much time went to each activity, how that compares with the plan, and how consistent the practice was. **Adherence** per activity is

$$\text{adherence}_k = \frac{\text{logged minutes}_k}{\text{planned minutes}_k} \times 100\%$$

and a **streak**, the number of consecutive days with practice, measures consistency. Streaks motivate, but they are a means, not an end: a missed day is followed by starting again, not by giving up.

### The weekly review

Each week ends with a short review: what share of planned tasks was completed, what carries over, and whether next week's load should change. A simple rule keeps the plan sustainable: if completion was below about 70 per cent, reduce next week's load by a fifth; if everything was completed, increase it by a tenth; otherwise keep it. Adjusting the plan to reality is a feature, not a failure.

### Adapting to your starting level

People start in different places. A quick **self-assessment** across six areas, prompting, tools, automation, agents, coding, and business, each scored from 0 (never done it) to 5 (could teach it), places you:

- Areas scored **4 or more** can be skimmed or skipped, using their weekly project as a test.
- Areas scored **0 or 1** are the focus: allocate extra build time there.
- The overall level, beginner, intermediate, or advanced, sets the ambition of the projects: an advanced learner builds an agent with tool use where a beginner builds a single automation.

Honest self-assessment matters; overrating yourself skips foundations you will need in week 3.

### Using the course modules inside the roadmap

The roadmap does not replace the modules; it sequences them. Each day names one module, or one section of a module, to study in the learning block, its video to watch at the point the module suggests, and a piece of the week's project to build with it. The module's lab is often the best first build: it is small, offline, and checked automatically, so it gives immediate feedback before you apply the idea to your own project. The module's Active Recall questions become the next day's review. If a day's material is already familiar, take the module's lab as a test: if it passes quickly, move on; if not, the module deserves the full time.

### Complexity

Budgets and placements are linear in activities or areas; log summaries are linear in entries; streaks require sorting the dates, $O(n \log n)$; the weekly review is linear in tasks.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, the daily time budget.

```python
from datetime import date

def daily_budget(weekly_hours, days, split):
    per_day = weekly_hours * 60 / days
    return {k: round(per_day * v) for k, v in split.items()}

SPLIT = {"learn": 0.4, "build": 0.45, "review": 0.15}
print(daily_budget(7, 6, SPLIT))
print(daily_budget(10, 5, {"learn": 0.3, "build": 0.6, "review": 0.1}))
```

- **Seven hours over six days: 28 minutes learning, 32 building, 10 reviewing.**
- **A colleague with ten hours over five days and more experience shifts towards building: 36, 72, and 12 minutes.**
- **Shares must sum to 1;** rounding can move a total by a minute.

Next, placement by starting level.

```python
AREAS = ["prompting", "tools", "automation", "agents", "coding", "business"]

def placement(scores, skip_at=4):
    skip = [a for a in AREAS if scores.get(a, 0) >= skip_at]
    focus = [a for a in AREAS if scores.get(a, 0) < 2]
    level = "advanced" if len(skip) >= 4 else ("intermediate" if len(skip) >= 2 else "beginner")
    return level, skip, focus

print(placement({"prompting": 4, "tools": 4, "automation": 2, "agents": 1, "coding": 3, "business": 0}))
print(placement({}))
```

- **Sam is intermediate: skim prompting and tools; focus on agents and business.**
- **Someone new to everything is a beginner with all six areas as focus;** the roadmap's default pace suits them.
- **Missing scores count as 0,** a cautious default.

Then, summarising the time log.

```python
def log_summary(log, plan):
    totals = {}
    for _, activity, minutes in log:
        totals[activity] = totals.get(activity, 0) + minutes
    adherence = {a: round(totals.get(a, 0) / m * 100) for a, m in plan.items()}
    return totals, adherence

LOG = [("2026-11-17", "learn", 30), ("2026-11-17", "build", 40), ("2026-11-18", "learn", 25),
       ("2026-11-19", "build", 50), ("2026-11-20", "review", 15)]
PLAN = {"learn": 168, "build": 189, "review": 63}
print(log_summary(LOG, PLAN))
```

- **Four days in, Sam has logged 55 minutes of learning, 90 of building, and 15 of review.**
- **Against a week's plan, adherence is 33, 48, and 24 per cent:** review is falling behind, a common pattern.
- **The weekly plan is the week's 420 minutes times each share: 168, 189, and 63.**

Then, practice streaks.

```python
def streaks(days):
    ds = sorted({date.fromisoformat(d) for d in days})
    if not ds:
        return 0, 0
    longest = cur = 1
    for a, b in zip(ds, ds[1:]):
        cur = cur + 1 if (b - a).days == 1 else 1
        longest = max(longest, cur)
    return cur, longest

print(streaks(["2026-11-10", "2026-11-11", "2026-11-12", "2026-11-14", "2026-11-15", "2026-11-16", "2026-11-17"]))
```

- **The current run is four days, 14 to 17 November; the longest is also four.** The gap on 13 November reset it.
- **Duplicate dates count once.** The current run is measured up to the last logged day.

Finally, the weekly review.

```python
def weekly_review(planned, done, load_hours):
    completed = [t for t in planned if t in done]
    rate = round(len(completed) / len(planned) * 100) if planned else 100
    carry = [t for t in planned if t not in done]
    if rate < 70:
        new_load = round(load_hours * 0.8, 1)
    elif rate >= 100:
        new_load = round(load_hours * 1.1, 1)
    else:
        new_load = load_hours
    return rate, carry, new_load

print(weekly_review(["day 1", "day 2", "day 3", "day 4", "day 5"], ["day 1", "day 2", "day 3"], 7))
print(weekly_review(["a", "b"], ["a", "b"], 7))
```

- **Three of five days completed, 60 per cent:** two carry over and next week's load drops to 5.6 hours.
- **Everything completed: load rises to 7.7 hours.**
- **The rule is deliberately simple;** the point is to adjust every week rather than abandon the plan.

**Complexity.** Linear apart from sorting dates.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Watching instead of building.** Videos feel productive. Build every day.

**Weekend cramming.** Spacing beats massing. Little and often.

**Skipping review.** It feels optional and is the first to go. Keep ten minutes.

**Rereading instead of recalling.** Answer questions without looking.

**Overrating your level.** Skipped foundations return in week 3.

**Open-ended sessions.** Timebox them.

**Breaking the chain and quitting.** Restart the next day.

**Never adjusting the plan.** Change the load after each weekly review.

**Too many tools at once.** Learn one well per week.

**Projects without deliverables.** Each week ends with something that works.

**No log.** You cannot review what you did not record.

**Comparing with others' progress.** Compare with your last week.
