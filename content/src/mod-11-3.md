# How to Identify Business Problems

The most valuable thing an AI builder can do for a business is not building; it is **finding the right problem**. Business owners rarely ask for "an agent" or "a RAG system"; they say "quoting takes forever" or "we lose enquiries". Turning a vague complaint into a specific, measured problem, with a cost in hours and money and a ranked list of fixes, is what earns trust, justifies a price, and avoids building clever things that do not matter. This module covers process mapping, finding bottlenecks, quantifying problems in time and money, and prioritising by impact and feasibility.

The joinery example and its figures are illustrative. Survey figures are from the UK Office for National Statistics, cited at the end, at the time of writing (November 2026).

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### Following a job through the workshop

To find out why jobs are late in a workshop, you do not ask people what they think; you **follow a job** from the moment the order arrives to the moment it leaves, writing down each step, how long someone actually works on it, and how long it sits waiting. Almost always, the job spends most of its life **waiting**, and one step, the **bottleneck**, holds everything up. Fixing other steps changes nothing until the bottleneck is fixed.

```text
 enquiry --> read email --> measure from drawings --> price materials --> write quote --> chase reply --> decision
 touch time:   4 min           25 min                   20 min             15 min          5 min
 waiting:      6 h             24 h                     4 h                2 h             72 h
 per week:     60              30                       30                 30              20
 errors:       -               5% (GBP 120 each)        8% (GBP 60 each)   2% (GBP 40)     -

 touch time 69 min, lead time about 109 h: the work is quick, the waiting is long
 most hours: measuring (12.5 h/week)    longest wait: chasing replies (72 h)
```

### Two kinds of problem

**Cost problems** consume staff hours and cause errors: they show up in the touch time, volumes, and error rates. **Speed problems** lose business because responses are slow: they show up in waiting time. A quote that takes 69 minutes of work but arrives four days later may lose the job to a faster competitor. Both are worth money, and they need different fixes.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### Process mapping

A process map lists the **steps** from trigger to outcome. For each step, record:

- **Touch time**: minutes someone actually works on one item.
- **Wait time**: how long the item waits before or after the step.
- **Volume**: how many items pass through per week.
- **Error rate and error cost**: how often the step goes wrong and what a mistake costs: a re-quote, a refund, a lost job.

Collect these by watching, asking for examples, and checking records such as email timestamps, not by asking for opinions. Value-stream mapping, from lean manufacturing, formalises the same idea.

### Lead time, touch time, and bottlenecks

**Lead time** is the total time from start to finish, waiting included; **touch time** is the work. The ratio shows how much of the process is waiting:

$$\text{lead time} = \sum_i (\text{wait}_i + \text{touch}_i), \qquad \text{flow efficiency} = \frac{\sum_i \text{touch}_i}{\text{lead time}}$$

For the joinery quote, 69 minutes of work in about 109 hours is a flow efficiency near 1 per cent, typical of office processes. The **theory of constraints** says a process's throughput is limited by its slowest step; improving any other step does not increase output. Little's law connects the quantities: the average number of items in progress equals the arrival rate times the average lead time, so long waits mean many quotes sitting half-done.

Two bottlenecks matter separately: the step with the most **staff hours per week**, touch time times volume, is the cost bottleneck; the step with the longest **wait** is the speed bottleneck.

### Quantifying in time and money

The annual cost of a step is its labour plus its errors:

$$\text{cost} = \underbrace{\frac{t \times v \times W}{60} \times h}_{\text{labour}} + \underbrace{e \times v \times W \times c_e}_{\text{errors}}$$

with touch time $t$ in minutes, weekly volume $v$, working weeks $W$, hourly staff cost $h$, error rate $e$, and cost per error $c_e$. At GBP 30 an hour over 48 weeks, measuring from drawings costs GBP 18,000 in labour and GBP 8,640 in errors, GBP 26,640 a year. Owners rarely know this number; putting it in front of them changes the conversation from "how much does AI cost?" to "how much is this problem costing me?"

### The automation business case

Automation rarely removes a step entirely. A realistic case assumes a **share** of the labour is automated and a share of errors avoided, minus running costs:

$$\text{annual saving} = \text{labour} \times s + \text{errors} \times r - 12 \times \text{monthly run cost}, \qquad \text{payback months} = \frac{\text{build cost}}{\text{saving} / 12}$$

Automating 70 per cent of material pricing and avoiding 75 per cent of its errors, for a GBP 2,500 build and GBP 20 a month to run, saves about GBP 15,000 a year and pays back in two months. Automating 30 per cent of measuring, harder because it means reading drawings, for GBP 6,000 pays back in eleven months. Payback is the number owners understand best.

### Prioritising by impact and feasibility

With several opportunities, rank them by expected value per unit of effort. A variant of the RICE method, which multiplies reach, impact, and confidence and divides by effort, works well:

$$\text{priority} = \frac{\text{annual saving} \times \text{confidence}}{\text{effort days}}$$

**Confidence** captures feasibility and uncertainty: a reliable automation of structured data might be 0.8; reading hand-drawn plans, 0.4. A quick, certain fix with modest savings, such as automatic reply chasing, can outrank a large, uncertain one.

### Writing the problem statement

A good problem statement names **who**, **what step**, **how much time**, and **what it costs**, without proposing a solution: "The estimator spends 12.5 hours a week measuring from drawings, costing about GBP 26,640 a year." Solutions come after the owner agrees the problem is real and worth solving.

### Complexity

Process statistics and costs are linear in the number of steps; prioritising $n$ opportunities is $O(n \log n)$.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, process statistics.

```python
import math

STEPS = [
    {"name": "read enquiry email", "minutes": 4, "wait_hours": 6, "per_week": 60, "error_rate": 0.0, "error_cost": 0},
    {"name": "measure from drawings", "minutes": 25, "wait_hours": 24, "per_week": 30, "error_rate": 0.05, "error_cost": 120},
    {"name": "price materials", "minutes": 20, "wait_hours": 4, "per_week": 30, "error_rate": 0.08, "error_cost": 60},
    {"name": "write quote", "minutes": 15, "wait_hours": 2, "per_week": 30, "error_rate": 0.02, "error_cost": 40},
    {"name": "chase reply", "minutes": 5, "wait_hours": 72, "per_week": 20, "error_rate": 0.0, "error_cost": 0},
]

def process_stats(steps):
    touch = sum(s["minutes"] for s in steps)
    lead_h = sum(s["wait_hours"] for s in steps) + touch / 60
    weekly_h = sum(s["minutes"] * s["per_week"] for s in steps) / 60
    by_time = max(steps, key=lambda s: s["minutes"] * s["per_week"])["name"]
    by_wait = max(steps, key=lambda s: s["wait_hours"])["name"]
    return {"touch_min": touch, "lead_hours": round(lead_h, 2), "weekly_hours": round(weekly_h, 2),
            "most_time": by_time, "longest_wait": by_wait}

print(process_stats(STEPS))
```

- **69 minutes of work, about 109 hours of lead time.** Most of a quote's life is waiting.
- **35.67 staff hours a week go on quoting,** nearly a full-time job.
- **Measuring takes the most time; chasing has the longest wait.** Two bottlenecks, two different fixes.

Next, the annual cost of each step.

```python
def annual_cost(step, hourly_cost, weeks=48):
    labour = step["minutes"] * step["per_week"] * weeks / 60 * hourly_cost
    errors = step["error_rate"] * step["per_week"] * weeks * step["error_cost"]
    return round(labour, 2), round(errors, 2), round(labour + errors, 2)

for s in STEPS:
    print(s["name"], annual_cost(s, 30))
```

- **Measuring costs GBP 26,640 a year; pricing GBP 21,312.** Errors add a third or more to labour for those two steps.
- **Reading emails costs GBP 5,760,** high volume, low time.
- **Hourly cost should include employer costs,** not just wages; GBP 30 is illustrative.

Then, the automation business case.

```python
def automation_case(step, hourly_cost, share, error_cut, build_cost, monthly_run, weeks=48):
    labour, errors, _ = annual_cost(step, hourly_cost, weeks)
    saving = labour * share + errors * error_cut - monthly_run * 12
    payback = None if saving <= 0 else round(build_cost / (saving / 12), 1)
    return round(saving, 2), payback

print(automation_case(STEPS[2], 30, 0.7, 0.75, 2500, 20))
print(automation_case(STEPS[1], 30, 0.3, 0.2, 6000, 50))
print(automation_case(STEPS[4], 30, 0.8, 0.0, 300, 0))
```

- **Pricing: GBP 15,024 a year, payback in 2 months.**
- **Measuring: GBP 6,528 a year, payback in 11 months,** because only 30 per cent can realistically be automated.
- **Chasing: GBP 1,920 a year for GBP 300, payback in 1.9 months,** and it also attacks the speed bottleneck.

Then, prioritising.

```python
def prioritise(opps):
    scored = [(o["name"], round(o["annual_saving"] * o["confidence"] / o["effort_days"], 1)) for o in opps]
    return sorted(scored, key=lambda x: -x[1])

OPPS = [{"name": "price materials", "annual_saving": 15024, "confidence": 0.8, "effort_days": 5},
        {"name": "measure from drawings", "annual_saving": 6528, "confidence": 0.4, "effort_days": 15},
        {"name": "chase reply", "annual_saving": 1920, "confidence": 0.9, "effort_days": 1}]
print(prioritise(OPPS))
```

- **Pricing first, then chasing, then measuring.**
- **Chasing ranks second despite small savings,** because it takes a day and is nearly certain.
- **Measuring ranks last:** large problem, uncertain fix, long build. Revisit it once trust is established.

Finally, the problem statement.

```python
def problem_statement(team, step, hourly_cost, weeks=48):
    hours = step["minutes"] * step["per_week"] / 60
    _, _, total = annual_cost(step, hourly_cost, weeks)
    return f"{team} spends {hours:.1f} hours a week on '{step['name']}', costing about GBP {total:,.0f} a year."

print(problem_statement("The estimator", STEPS[1], 30))
print(problem_statement("The estimator", STEPS[2], 30))
```

- **No solution is mentioned.** The owner first agrees the problem and its size.
- **Hours and money together** speak to both the people doing the work and the person paying.

**Complexity.** Linear in steps; sorting for priority.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Starting with the solution.** "You need an agent" before understanding the process. Map first.

**Asking for opinions instead of observing.** People misremember durations. Use timestamps and examples.

**Ignoring waiting time.** Most lead time is waiting. Measure it.

**Optimising a non-bottleneck.** Faster email reading does not speed quotes. Find the constraint.

**Leaving out errors.** Rework and lost jobs are often the bigger cost.

**Assuming full automation.** Most steps are partly automatable. Use a realistic share.

**Forgetting running costs.** APIs, hosting, and maintenance reduce savings, Module 11.12.

**Over-precise numbers.** Estimates are estimates. Round and state assumptions.

**Big-bang projects first.** Start with quick, certain wins to build trust.

**Ignoring people.** The estimator may fear replacement. Frame automation as removing drudgery.

**Not confirming with the owner.** The problem must be theirs, in their words.

**Small-business adoption.** Many small firms do not yet use AI. Lead with the problem, not the technology.
