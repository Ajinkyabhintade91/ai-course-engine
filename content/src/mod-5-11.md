# Prompting for Images

Every prompt so far in this unit has been addressed to a model that writes. This chapter is about models that draw. The habits carry over only in part. An image model does not follow instructions in the way a language model does, cannot ask what you meant, and responds to words through a mechanism that is worth understanding, because it explains nearly every surprise.

The chapter covers that mechanism, a formula for writing image prompts, and the controls that sit beside the text: negative prompts, reference images, seeds, and the methods for getting the same character or style twice. Module 6.2 covers the image tools themselves and the production workflow. This chapter is about the words.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### A caption for a picture that does not exist yet

Image models learn from an enormous number of pictures paired with text describing them: captions, titles, alternative text. They learn which words go with which pictures.

That gives the most useful single idea in this chapter. **An image prompt is a caption, not a command.** You are writing the description that would sit under the finished picture, and the model produces a picture that such a description would fit.

Compare two prompts.

| Written as a command | Written as a caption |
|---|---|
| "Please create an image for my bakery's website. Make it warm and inviting, and do not include any people." | "A wooden counter with fresh sourdough loaves and croissants in warm morning light, empty bakery interior, photograph, shallow depth of field." |

The first is addressed to an assistant. It names a purpose and states a wish. It contains almost nothing that could be seen. The second describes a picture.

### The photographer by post

Imagine commissioning a photograph by letter from someone you will never speak to. They cannot ask whether you wanted a close-up, what time of day, which way the subject faces. Whatever the letter leaves out, they decide.

A good letter would say what is in the picture, what kind of picture it is, where the camera is, what the light is like, and what style to work in. Those are the parts of the image prompt formula.

### What happens to your words

```text
   YOUR PROMPT
       |
       v
   TEXT ENCODER          turns the words into vectors of numbers
       |
       v                                         SEED
   CONDITIONING  ------------------+              |
                                   v              v
                           +----------------------------+
                           |  start from random noise   |
                           |  remove a little noise     |  repeated
                           |  in the direction the      |  20 to 50
                           |  text points               |  times
                           +----------------------------+
                                        |
                                        v
                                     IMAGE
```

The picture begins as random noise and is cleaned up in steps. At each step the model looks at the noisy picture and at the numbers that represent your text, and nudges the picture towards something those numbers describe. Your words are a direction to steer in.

Two consequences follow before any detail. Words that describe visible things steer well, and words that describe purposes or instructions steer poorly. And the starting noise matters: the same prompt from different noise gives a different picture.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### From text to conditioning

A diffusion image model has three parts.

A **text encoder** turns the prompt into a sequence of vectors. Early models used the encoder from CLIP, a model trained to match pictures with their captions. Later ones add or substitute a larger language encoder that reads long sentences better.

A **denoiser** does the generating. It is trained by taking real images, adding noise, and learning to predict the noise that was added, given the caption. To generate, it starts from pure noise and repeatedly removes its prediction of the noise. At each step, a mechanism called cross-attention lets every region of the picture consult the text vectors.

A **decoder** turns the result into pixels. Most models work on a compressed version of the image to save computation: a picture of 512 by 512 pixels is handled as a grid of 64 by 64 with four channels. This design, from a 2021 paper on latent diffusion, is what made the models cheap enough for wide use.

### Guidance: how strongly the text is obeyed

Left alone, the denoiser follows the text only loosely. The technique that makes prompts effective is **classifier-free guidance**, introduced in a 2022 paper.

At each step the model makes two predictions of the noise: one with your prompt, $\epsilon_{c}$, and one with an empty prompt, $\epsilon_{u}$. The difference between them is the effect of your words. Guidance exaggerates that difference by a factor $w$, the guidance scale:

$$
\hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_{u} + w \, (\epsilon_{c} - \epsilon_{u})
$$

With $w = 1$ the result is simply the prompted prediction. With $w$ larger, the picture is pushed further in the direction that distinguishes "with the prompt" from "without it".

| Guidance scale | Effect |
|---|---|
| Low | Loose reading of the prompt, more variety, softer images |
| Middle | The usual range: the prompt is followed and the image stays natural |
| High | Literal reading, with harsh contrast, oversaturated colour, and artefacts |

The useful numbers differ between models, and some newer models have guidance built in and expose no setting. Where a setting exists, the lesson is that turning it up is not the way to make a model listen. Past the middle of the range, the picture gets worse.

### What a negative prompt does

A negative prompt replaces the empty prompt in the formula. If $\epsilon_{n}$ is the prediction for the negative prompt:

$$
\hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_{n} + w \, (\epsilon_{c} - \epsilon_{n})
$$

Each step now moves away from what the negative prompt describes and towards what the prompt describes. It is arithmetic on vectors, and it is the only reliable form of "not" these models have.

This explains the commonest failure in image prompting. Writing "no people" in the main prompt puts the word "people" into the text vectors. The encoder represents negation weakly, the vectors now contain people, and people appear. The same request in the negative prompt subtracts the direction instead of adding it.

Two rules follow. **Describe what you want to see**, in the positive: "an empty street" and not "a street with no cars". And where a tool has a negative prompt field, **put exclusions there**, as plain nouns: "people, text, watermark".

### The seed

The starting noise is produced by a random number generator, and the **seed** is the number that initialises it. The same seed, prompt, model version, and settings give the same picture.

| Use | How |
|---|---|
| Explore | Keep the prompt, change the seed: different pictures of the same description |
| Refine | Keep the seed, change one phrase: the effect of that phrase, with the composition roughly held |
| Reproduce | Record the seed, the exact prompt, the model version, and the settings |

The second use is the image-prompting form of the rule from Module 5.9: change one thing at a time. With a fixed seed, a change in the picture is due to the change in the prompt. Without one, you cannot tell your edit from the dice. A seed is not portable: a new model version gives a different picture from the same number.

### The length limit

The CLIP text encoder reads at most 77 tokens, including a start and an end marker. Tools built on it either cut the prompt off there or split it into chunks with weaker effect. Whatever comes after the limit may be ignored entirely, with no warning.

Models that use a larger language encoder accept prompts of several hundred tokens. Even there, attention to a long prompt is uneven. The rule for all of them is to put what matters most first. Module 5.3 gave the fuller version for language models: the start and the end of an input are used best, and the middle least.

### Two families of image model

| | Diffusion models with settings | Multimodal chat models |
|---|---|---|
| Examples at the time of writing (November 2026) | Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, FLUX | Image generation inside ChatGPT and Gemini |
| How you prompt | A caption, plus parameters | A description, in conversation |
| Negative prompt | A separate field or parameter, in many tools | Usually none: describe what you want |
| Editing | Generate again, or mask a region | Ask in words: "keep everything, make the sky overcast" |
| Text inside the image | Historically weak, improving | Much stronger |
| Seeds and settings | Exposed | Usually hidden |

The second family is built on a language model, so it understands instructions, follows long descriptions, and can revise an image over several turns. The caption habit still serves well there, because a full description of the picture is the clearest instruction there is. Vendor guides for both families give the same advice: write descriptive sentences, not lists of keywords.

### The formula

| Part | Question it answers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject** | What is in the picture, doing what? | "A red fox asleep in fresh snow" |
| **Medium** | What kind of picture is it? | Photograph, watercolour, pencil sketch, 3D render, vector icon |
| **Style** | In what manner? | Documentary, art deco, flat design, film noir |
| **Composition** | Where is the camera and how is the frame arranged? | Close-up, low angle, subject on the left third, wide shot from above |
| **Lighting** | What is the light like? | Soft morning light, hard noon sun, backlit, candlelit, overcast |
| **Parameters** | What shape, and which settings? | Aspect ratio, seed, guidance, negative prompt, reference images |

The subject comes first and gets the most words. The other parts each cut down the decisions left to the model. A prompt that names only a subject leaves the medium, the framing, and the light to chance, which is why the same short prompt gives such different pictures.

Photographic terms are the most efficient vocabulary for composition and light, because so many training captions used them.

| To control | Say |
|---|---|
| How much of the scene | Extreme close-up, close-up, medium shot, wide shot |
| Viewpoint | Eye level, low angle, high angle, overhead |
| Background blur | Shallow depth of field, 85mm lens. Or: deep focus, 24mm lens |
| Time and mood | Golden hour, blue hour, overcast, night with street lamps |
| Direction of light | Backlit, side-lit, lit from below |

Parameters are written differently in every tool. At the time of writing, Midjourney takes them as flags after the prompt, such as `--ar 16:9` for the aspect ratio, `--seed 42`, `--no text` for a negative prompt, and `--sref` followed by an image address for a style reference. The names change between versions. The ideas do not.

### What image models find hard

**Binding attributes to objects.** "A red cube and a blue sphere" sometimes comes back with the colours exchanged or shared. The text vectors do not firmly attach each adjective to its noun. Shorter prompts with fewer objects, and one clause per object, help.

**Counting.** "Seven apples" often gives six or nine. Above three or four, do not rely on a number.

**Text.** Lettering was long a weakness of diffusion models and is now handled well by the newest models of both families. Put the exact words in quotation marks, and keep them short.

**Spatial relations.** "To the left of", "behind", and "underneath" are followed less reliably than what the objects are.

**Negation**, as explained above.

### Reference images and consistency

Words cannot pin down a face or a style precisely. Images can. Most tools accept one or more of these.

| Reference | Carries over |
|---|---|
| An image prompt | General content and look |
| A style reference | Palette, texture, and manner, without the content |
| A character or subject reference | A particular person, creature, or object |
| An image to transform | The composition, which the prompt then alters |

For a set of pictures that must belong together, three habits do most of the work. Keep an identical block of text for the character and for the style, and vary only the scene. Use a reference image of the character wherever the tool allows. And where seeds are exposed, hold the seed while making small changes. Module 6.2 returns to the tooling.

### Rights, briefly

Three points belong in a chapter on prompting. In the United States, copyright requires human authorship, and the Copyright Office's position is that a prompt alone does not give its writer enough control over the result to make them its author. In March 2026 the Supreme Court declined to review the leading case on the question, which left that requirement standing. Second, naming a living artist in a prompt to imitate their style is legal ground that is still contested and is widely regarded as unfair to the artist. Describe the qualities you want instead. Third, a prompt that names a trademarked character or a real person carries the same risks as any other use of them. Module 6.2 covers licensing for commercial work.

### Complexity

A prompt's cost in an image model does not grow with its length the way a language model's does: an image is usually priced per picture or by resolution. The cost that grows is the number of attempts. Exploring $s$ seeds for each of $v$ variations takes $s \times v$ generations, which is why a plan for what to vary is worth writing first.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

First, assembling a prompt from its parts and checking it for the commonest mistakes.

```python
import re

ORDER = ("subject", "medium", "style", "composition", "lighting")
NEGATIONS = ("no", "not", "without", "never")
FILLER = ("8k", "4k", "masterpiece", "best quality", "ultra detailed", "award winning", "trending on artstation")

def build_image_prompt(parts):
    if not parts.get("subject", "").strip():
        raise ValueError("an image prompt needs a subject")
    clauses = [parts[name].strip().rstrip(".") for name in ORDER if parts.get(name, "").strip()]
    return ". ".join(clauses) + "."

def lint_image_prompt(text):
    words = " " + " ".join(re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9 ]", " ", text.lower()).split()) + " "
    problems = []
    if any(" " + word + " " in words for word in NEGATIONS):
        problems.append("negation")
    if any(" " + phrase + " " in words for phrase in FILLER):
        problems.append("filler")
    fragments = [fragment for fragment in text.split(",") if fragment.strip()]
    if len(fragments) >= 5 and sum(len(fragment.split()) for fragment in fragments) / len(fragments) < 3:
        problems.append("keyword-list")
    return problems

FOX = {
    "subject": "A red fox asleep in fresh snow at the edge of a pine forest",
    "medium": "Photograph, 85mm lens, shallow depth of field",
    "style": "Quiet wildlife documentary",
    "composition": "Close-up from a low angle, the fox on the left third of the frame",
    "lighting": "Soft blue light just before sunrise",
}
prompt = build_image_prompt(FOX)
print(prompt)
print(lint_image_prompt(prompt))
print(lint_image_prompt("city street, no people, 8k, masterpiece, not blurry, award winning, cinematic"))
print(lint_image_prompt("A knot of rope on a wooden dock"), lint_image_prompt("Portrait of a woman without glasses"))
```

- **`build_image_prompt` joins the parts in a fixed order, subject first.** The subject is the one part that is required. Each part becomes a short sentence, so the result reads as a description and not a list of tags.
- **Blank parts are left out.** A prompt with only a subject and a medium is valid. It simply leaves more to the model.
- **`lint_image_prompt` looks for three mistakes.** A negation word in the main prompt, which names the thing you wanted to exclude. A quality filler such as "8k" or "masterpiece", which describes nothing visible. And a keyword list: five or more comma-separated fragments averaging under three words each.
- **The fox prompt has no findings.** It is five sentences of visible detail.
- **The second prompt has all three.** "No people" and "not blurry" are negations, "8k", "masterpiece", and "award winning" are filler, and the whole is seven short fragments. Rewritten, it would say "an empty city street, sharp focus", with "people, blur" in the negative prompt.
- **Whole words only.** "Knot" contains "not" and is not flagged. "Without glasses" is. The cure there is a negative prompt, or simply describing the face and leaving glasses unmentioned.

Next, what guidance and a negative prompt do to the numbers.

```python
def guide(base, conditioned, scale):
    return [round(b + scale * (c - b), 4) for b, c in zip(base, conditioned)]

empty = [0.0, 0.0]
with_prompt = [1.0, 0.5]
negative = [0.4, -0.2]
print(guide(empty, with_prompt, 1), guide(empty, with_prompt, 7.5), guide(empty, with_prompt, 0))
print(guide(negative, with_prompt, 7.5))
```

- **`guide` is the formula from Section 2, applied to each number in turn.** It starts from the base prediction and moves along the difference between the conditioned prediction and the base, multiplied by the scale. Two numbers stand in for the many thousands in a real prediction.
- **At a scale of 1, the result is the prompted prediction.** At 0 it is the base, and the prompt has no effect. At 7.5 the prompt's effect is exaggerated seven and a half times: 7.5 and 3.75.
- **With a negative prompt as the base, the result changes to 4.9 and 5.05.** Compare each number with the unguided 7.5 and 3.75.
- **Where the negative prompt pointed the opposite way, the push grew.** Its second number was minus 0.2. Moving away from it raised the result from 3.75 to 5.05.
- **Where the negative prompt pointed the same way as the prompt, the push shrank.** Its first number was 0.4, in the same direction as the prompt's 1.0. The result fell from 7.5 to 4.9. A negative prompt that shares features with your subject weakens the subject, which is why "blurry background" as a negative can flatten a portrait that was meant to have one.

Then, planning variations so that each change can be read.

```python
def variation_grid(parts, field, options, seeds):
    grid = []
    for option in options:
        for seed in seeds:
            changed = dict(parts)
            changed[field] = option
            grid.append({"seed": seed, "changed": option, "parts": changed})
    return grid

grid = variation_grid(FOX, "lighting", ["Soft blue light just before sunrise", "Warm low sun at golden hour", "Heavy overcast, falling snow"], [11, 42])
print(len(grid))
for cell in grid[:3]:
    print(cell["seed"], "|", build_image_prompt(cell["parts"])[-40:])
print(FOX["lighting"])
```

- **`variation_grid` changes one part and nothing else.** Every cell is a copy of the original parts with a single field replaced, paired with a seed.
- **Each option is run on each seed.** Three kinds of light on two seeds is six generations. Looking along a row shows the effect of the light with the noise held. Looking down a column shows how much is due to the seed.
- **The original parts are not altered.** The function copies before it changes, and the last line confirms that the fox's lighting is as it was.
- **The grid is a budget as well as a plan.** Options times seeds is the number of images to be paid for. Writing the grid first is how the exploring stays deliberate.

Finally, what a token limit cuts off.

```python
def token_budget(text, limit=77):
    pieces = re.findall(r"\w+|[^\w\s]", text)
    room = limit - 2
    return {"tokens": len(pieces) + 2, "fits": len(pieces) <= room, "dropped": " ".join(pieces[room:])}

short = ("A red fox asleep in fresh snow at the edge of a pine forest. Photograph, 85mm lens, shallow depth of field. "
         "Soft blue light just before sunrise, long shadows. Close-up from a low angle, the fox fills the left third of the frame.")
long = short + (" The trees behind are dusted with snow and slightly out of focus, a faint mist hangs between the trunks, "
                "and a single set of paw prints leads from the lower right corner towards the animal. "
                "The fox must have a white tip on its tail.")
print(token_budget(short))
print(token_budget(long))
```

- **The count is an estimate.** Each word and each punctuation mark is counted as one token, and two are added for the start and end markers. A real tokenizer splits some words into more than one piece, so the true count is a little higher.
- **The short prompt is 55 tokens and fits.**
- **The long prompt is 104 tokens, and 27 of them are dropped.** The text from "a single set of paw prints" onwards is beyond the limit.
- **The most important requirement was last.** "The fox must have a white tip on its tail" is the sentence the author cared about, and on a model with this limit it is never read. Nothing reports the loss. The picture simply lacks the detail.
- **The cure is order.** Requirements go at the front, atmosphere at the back, and anything that cannot fit is cut by you and not by the encoder.

**Complexity.** `build_image_prompt`, `lint_image_prompt`, and `token_budget` are linear in the text. `guide` is linear in the length of the vectors. `variation_grid` produces options times seeds cells.

### An image prompt template

```text
[SUBJECT: who or what, doing what, where]
[MEDIUM: photograph, watercolour, vector icon, 3D render]
[STYLE: the manner, described by its qualities]
[COMPOSITION: shot size, viewpoint, where the subject sits in the frame]
[LIGHTING: time of day, direction, quality]

Negative prompt (if the tool has one): [nouns to exclude]
Aspect ratio: [for example 16:9]        Seed: [recorded]
Reference images: [character, style]    Model and version: [recorded]
```

### Fixing a picture

| What is wrong | Change |
|---|---|
| The thing you excluded appears | Remove it from the prompt. Use the negative prompt |
| The framing is wrong | Add shot size and viewpoint |
| It looks like a generic stock image | Add a specific medium, lens, and light |
| Colours or details land on the wrong object | Fewer objects, one clause each |
| A late detail is ignored | Move it to the front. Shorten the prompt |
| Harsh, oversaturated result | Lower the guidance scale |
| The character changes between images | Identical description block, plus a reference image |
| You cannot tell whether an edit helped | Fix the seed and change one thing |

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Talking to the image model like a chatbot.** "Can you make me a logo for my café that feels friendly" is a request to an assistant. A diffusion model reads it as a caption, and little of it is visible. Chat-based image models cope better, and still do best with a description of the picture.

**"No" in the prompt.** The most reported surprise in image prompting: ask for no text and get text, ask for no people and get a crowd. The word is in the prompt, so the thing is in the picture. Use the negative field, or describe the scene in the positive.

**Quality incantations.** "8k, masterpiece, best quality, trending on artstation" were habits formed on early models, where such tags had some effect. On current models they do little, and they spend the limited tokens that the encoder reads. Describe the medium and the light.

**Keyword soup.** Lists of comma-separated tags suit some older models trained on tagged images. Current models, and the vendors' own guides, favour sentences, which carry the relations between things: who is holding what, what is behind what.

**The prompt that is too long.** Three hundred words of description, and the model follows the first third. On encoders with a 77-token limit the rest is cut off. Elsewhere it is diluted. Put the essentials first and cut the rest.

**Turning guidance up to make it listen.** A higher scale makes the picture harsher, not more obedient. If the model ignores part of the prompt, the cause is usually order, length, or binding.

**Too many subjects.** Each additional object raises the chance that attributes are exchanged between them. For a complex scene, generate the main composition and then edit regions, as Module 6.2 describes.

**Expecting a count.** Asking for exactly eleven candles and regenerating until it happens costs more than adding them in an editor.

**Chasing a good image without the seed.** A picture that was nearly right, generated with a random seed that nobody recorded, cannot be refined, only replaced. Record the seed, the prompt, and the model version for anything worth keeping.

**A seed carried to a new version.** The same number on an updated model gives a different picture. Reproducibility lasts as long as the model does.

**Character drift.** A character described slightly differently in each prompt becomes a different person in each picture. Write the description once and paste it unchanged, and use a reference image.

**Naming living artists.** It often works, and it is the practice most objected to by the people whose work trained the models. Describing the qualities, such as loose ink line and flat pastel colour, is fairer, more portable between tools, and gives you a style that is yours to describe.

**Assuming you own the result.** A picture made from a prompt alone may have no copyright owner at all in some countries, which means a competitor could use it too. For work that matters commercially, check the tool's terms and the law where you trade.

## 5. Active Recall Check & Synthesis Summary

**Summary.** An image model is trained on pictures paired with captions, so an image prompt works as a caption for a picture that does not exist yet, and not as a command. A text encoder turns the words into vectors, and a denoiser starts from random noise and removes it in steps, steered by those vectors. Classifier-free guidance sets how strongly: $\hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_{u} + w(\epsilon_{c} - \epsilon_{u})$, where a larger $w$ follows the prompt more literally and, past a point, damages the image. A negative prompt takes the place of the unconditioned prediction, so that each step moves away from it. That is why exclusions belong there and why "no" in the main prompt tends to produce the thing named. The seed fixes the starting noise: change it to explore, hold it to see the effect of one edit. Encoders have length limits, so the essentials go first. The formula is subject, medium, style, composition, lighting, and parameters, written as descriptive sentences with photographic vocabulary. Models remain weak at binding attributes, counting, spatial relations, and negation. Consistency across images comes from an identical description block, reference images, and a held seed.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 1
def build_image_prompt(parts):
    """A caption for a picture that does not exist yet: the subject first, then each part
    as a short descriptive sentence. Whatever is left out, the model decides."""
    if not parts.get("subject", "").strip():
        raise ValueError("an image prompt needs a subject")
    clauses = [parts[name].strip().rstrip(".") for name in ORDER if parts.get(name, "").strip()]
    return ". ".join(clauses) + "."


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 2
def lint_image_prompt(text):
    """A negation names the thing to be excluded, a filler describes nothing visible, and a
    keyword list carries no relations between things."""
    words = " " + " ".join(re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9 ]", " ", text.lower()).split()) + " "
    problems = []
    if any(" " + word + " " in words for word in NEGATIONS):
        problems.append("negation")
    if any(" " + phrase + " " in words for phrase in FILLER):
        problems.append("filler")
    fragments = [fragment for fragment in text.split(",") if fragment.strip()]
    if len(fragments) >= 5 and sum(len(fragment.split()) for fragment in fragments) / len(fragments) < 3:
        problems.append("keyword-list")
    return problems


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 3
def guide(base, conditioned, scale):
    """Guidance exaggerates the difference the prompt makes. A negative prompt as the base
    strengthens what distinguishes the prompt from it and weakens what the two share."""
    return [round(b + scale * (c - b), 4) for b, c in zip(base, conditioned)]


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 4
def variation_grid(parts, field, options, seeds):
    """One part changed, everything else held, on several seeds. Along a row is the effect
    of the change. Down a column is the effect of the dice. The length is the budget."""
    grid = []
    for option in options:
        for seed in seeds:
            changed = dict(parts)
            changed[field] = option
            grid.append({"seed": seed, "changed": option, "parts": changed})
    return grid


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 5
def token_budget(text, limit=77):
    """An estimate: one token per word or punctuation mark. Whatever falls beyond the limit
    is never read, and nothing reports the loss, so requirements go first."""
    pieces = re.findall(r"\w+|[^\w\s]", text)
    room = limit - 2
    return {"tokens": len(pieces) + 2, "fits": len(pieces) <= room, "dropped": " ".join(pieces[room:])}
