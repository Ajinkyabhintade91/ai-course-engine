# The Future of AI

Predicting technology is a reliable way to look foolish. This chapter does not tell you what will happen. It gives you something more durable: the measured trends that are driving AI forward, the arithmetic for reasoning about them, the directions that are already visible, and an honest account of what is uncertain. With these you can evaluate a forecast yourself, which is worth more than any forecast.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### Three compounding curves

Progress in AI over the past decade came mainly from three things growing at once.

1. **Compute.** The amount of computation used to train the largest models has grown several-fold every year.
2. **Algorithms.** Researchers keep finding ways to get more from the same computation: better architectures, better training methods, better use of data.
3. **Cost of use.** The price of running a model at a given level of capability keeps falling, as hardware improves and smaller models match what larger ones used to do.

Each behaves like compound interest. Money at 7 percent interest doubles in about ten years. A quantity that grows 4 times a year doubles in six months. Human intuition is poor at compounding. We tend to imagine next year as this year plus a bit, when the trend says this year times four.

### Two different questions

Keep two things apart, because public discussion constantly blends them.

- **How capable is the best system?** This is the **frontier**, and it rises.
- **What does a fixed level of capability cost?** This falls.

```text
 capability                                        cost of a FIXED capability
     ^                             frontier            ^
     |                          .-'                    | \
     |                      .-'                        |  \
     |                  .-'                            |   \
     |              .-'                                |    '.
     |          .-'                                    |      '-.
     |      .-'                                        |         '--.__
     +------------------------------> time             +------------------> time
       yesterday's frontier becomes
       today's cheap commodity
```

The two curves are linked. A capability that needed the most expensive model two years ago is now available from a small, cheap one. For someone building products this second curve often matters more than the first: it decides what becomes affordable to do at scale.

### The bitter lesson

In 2019 the reinforcement-learning pioneer Rich Sutton wrote a short essay called "The Bitter Lesson". Looking back over seventy years of AI research, he observed that methods relying on human-designed knowledge of a domain repeatedly lost, in the long run, to general methods that could exploit more computation: search and learning. It is bitter because researchers' cleverness about the domain mattered less than scale.

The lesson is a useful prior for thinking about the future. Approaches that improve automatically as compute grows have tended to win. It is not a law of nature, and it says nothing about when a given approach hits its limits.

### Exponentials end

Here is the counterweight. Nothing in the physical world grows exponentially for ever. Growth that looks exponential is usually the early part of an **S-curve**: slow start, rapid middle, and a levelling off as some limit is approached, whether that limit is energy, money, data, or physics. The difficulty is that the early part of an S-curve and a true exponential look identical. You cannot tell from the data so far when the bend will come. Every forecast in this field is a bet about where on the curve we are.

## 2. Core Theory, Mathematical Foundations & Architecture Internals

### The mathematics of growth

If a quantity starts at $x_0$ and multiplies by a **growth factor** $g$ every period, then after $t$ periods:

$$
x(t) = x_0 \, g^{\,t}
$$

The **doubling time** $T_d$ is the time needed for the quantity to double. Setting $g^{T_d} = 2$ and taking logarithms:

$$
T_d = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln g}
$$

and in the other direction, $g = 2^{1/T_d}$. Some reference values:

| Growth per year | Doubling time |
|---|---|
| 1.07 (7 percent interest) | about 10.2 years |
| 1.41 | 2 years |
| 2 | 1 year |
| 4 | 6 months |
| 5 | about 5.2 months |
| 10 | about 3.6 months |

To ask how long until a quantity reaches a target, solve $x_0 g^{t} = x_{\text{target}}$:

$$
t = \frac{\ln\left(x_{\text{target}} / x_0\right)}{\ln g}
$$

To find the per-year factor from two measurements $\Delta t$ years apart:

$$
g = \left(\frac{x_1}{x_0}\right)^{1/\Delta t}
$$

**Fitting a trend.** Real data is noisy, so a trend is estimated from many points. Taking logarithms turns an exponential into a straight line:

$$
\ln x(t) = \ln x_0 + t \,\ln g
$$

So we fit a line $\ell = a + b\,t$ to the points $(t_i, \ln x_i)$ by least squares. With $\bar{t}$ and $\bar{\ell}$ the averages:

$$
b = \frac{\sum_i (t_i - \bar{t})(\ell_i - \bar{\ell})}{\sum_i (t_i - \bar{t})^{2}}
\qquad
a = \bar{\ell} - b\,\bar{t}
$$

and then $g = e^{b}$ and $x_0 = e^{a}$. This is exactly how the trend lines in AI progress reports are produced. It is also how a small amount of noise in a few data points becomes a confident-looking line.

**The S-curve.** The standard model of growth that saturates is the logistic function, with ceiling $L$, steepness $k$, and midpoint $t_m$:

$$
x(t) = \frac{L}{1 + e^{-k\,(t - t_m)}}
$$

Well before the midpoint it grows almost exactly exponentially, at rate $k$. At the midpoint it has reached half the ceiling. After that, growth slows and the curve flattens toward $L$.

### The measured trends

**Training compute.** The research organisation Epoch AI, which tracks these figures, reports that the computation used to train frontier models has grown by about 4 to 5 times per year. Using the formula, a factor of 4.5 corresponds to a doubling time of $\ln 2 / \ln 4.5 \approx 0.46$ years, about five and a half months. For comparison, Moore's law, the famous observation about the density of transistors on chips, describes a doubling roughly every two years. AI training compute has grown far faster than chips improve, which means the difference has been made up by spending more money and building larger data centres. That is one reason the trend cannot continue indefinitely at this rate.

**Cost of use.** Stanford's AI Index report for 2025 documented that the cost of querying a model performing at the level of GPT-3.5 fell from about USD 20 per million tokens in November 2022 to about USD 0.07 in October 2024, a drop of more than 280-fold in roughly 23 months. Annualising with the formula above:

$$
280^{\,1/1.92} \approx 19
$$

so that level of capability became about 19 times cheaper per year over the period. Estimates of this rate vary enormously depending on which capability level is tracked, with published figures ranging from under 10 times to several hundred times per year. Treat the direction as solid and any single number as illustrative.

**Task length.** The research group METR measures the length of tasks, in human working time, that AI systems can complete with 50 percent reliability. Their 2025 paper found this horizon had been doubling about every seven months since 2019, reaching roughly 50 minutes for the best model at the time. As an illustration of the arithmetic only: going from 50 minutes to one working month of about 167 hours is a factor of about 200, which is $\log_2 200 \approx 7.6$ doublings, or about 54 months at that pace. The paper's own extrapolation places that milestone somewhere between 2028 and 2031, and its authors caution that the measurement covers well-specified software tasks and may not generalise. This is among the most contested extrapolations in the field.

### Near-term directions already under way

| Direction | What is changing | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Agents | Models act through tools over many steps | Moves AI from answering to doing; reliability is the bottleneck |
| Reasoning models | Models spend computation thinking before answering | Capability can be bought at the time of use, not only at training time |
| Multimodality | One model handles text, images, audio, and video | Opens up work that lives in documents, screens, and speech |
| On-device models | Small models run on phones and laptops | Privacy, offline use, and zero marginal cost |
| Lower cost | Fixed capability keeps getting cheaper | Makes high-volume uses economical |

Notice that the second row changes the scaling picture. If performance improves when a model is given more time to think, then compute matters at inference as well as in training, and the price of a hard answer can be traded against its quality.

### Economics and jobs

The most useful distinction here is between **exposure** and **displacement**. A job is exposed if AI could perform or assist with a meaningful share of its tasks. That says nothing yet about whether the job disappears, changes, or becomes more productive.

- The International Monetary Fund estimated in January 2024 that about 40 percent of jobs worldwide are exposed to AI, rising to about 60 percent in advanced economies, and judged that roughly half of the exposed jobs could benefit from AI raising productivity while the other half face reduced demand for labour.
- The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025*, based on employer surveys, projected about 170 million roles created and 92 million displaced by 2030, a net gain of 78 million, with large shifts in the skills required.

Both are projections built on assumptions, and they measure different things. The pattern they share is consistent with history: technology automates tasks more than whole occupations, and the mix of tasks within jobs shifts. Early evidence on effects for entry-level workers in highly exposed fields is mixed and its interpretation is disputed. Be wary of anyone claiming certainty in either direction.

A second economic effect is worth knowing by name. The **Jevons paradox** observes that when a resource becomes cheaper to use, total consumption of it often rises. Cheaper tokens have so far led to far more tokens being used, especially by agents and reasoning models, so falling prices have not meant falling total spending.

### Regulation

The European Union's **AI Act** is the first comprehensive law of its kind. It classifies AI systems by risk.

| Risk tier | Examples | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Unacceptable | Social scoring by governments, manipulative systems that exploit vulnerabilities | Banned |
| High | AI used in hiring, credit scoring, education, medical devices, critical infrastructure | Strict obligations: risk management, documentation, human oversight, accuracy testing |
| Limited | Chatbots, AI-generated media | Transparency: people must be told they are dealing with AI or synthetic content |
| Minimal | Spam filters, AI in video games | No new obligations |

General-purpose AI models, which include large language models, have their own set of obligations. The law entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. The bans applied from February 2025 and the obligations for general-purpose models from August 2025. The rules for high-risk systems were originally scheduled for August 2026, and a simplification package known as the Digital Omnibus moved to postpone them, to late 2027 for stand-alone high-risk systems. Dates in this area have shifted more than once, so check a current source before advising a client. Penalties for the most serious violations can reach 35 million euros or 7 percent of worldwide annual turnover.

In the United States, the National Institute of Standards and Technology published a voluntary **AI Risk Management Framework** in January 2023. It organises the work into four functions: **Govern** (set up accountability and policy), **Map** (understand the context and the risks), **Measure** (test and monitor), and **Manage** (act on what is found). Many organisations use it as a checklist even where no law requires it.

### Safety as engineering

AI safety is sometimes presented as a philosophical debate. In practice a large and growing part of it is ordinary engineering, with the same shape as safety work in aviation or medicine: define what must not happen, test for it before release, monitor in operation, report incidents, and improve. Concretely this includes evaluations for dangerous capabilities, red-teaming in which people try to make a system misbehave, interpretability research into what happens inside models, and operational controls such as the permission systems and approval gates you met in Module 1.6. If you build with AI you will be doing a small version of this work, whether or not you call it safety.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

The formulas from Section 2 make a compact toolkit. Standard library only.

```python
import math

def doubling_time(growth_factor):
    """Periods needed to double, for a quantity multiplying by growth_factor each period."""
    return math.log(2) / math.log(growth_factor)

def project(value, growth_factor, periods):
    """Value after compounding for the given number of periods."""
    return value * growth_factor ** periods

def periods_until(value, target, growth_factor):
    """How many periods until value reaches target."""
    return math.log(target / value) / math.log(growth_factor)

def fit_exponential(points):
    """Least-squares fit of x = x0 * g**t to (t, x) points. Returns (x0, g)."""
    ts = [t for t, _ in points]
    logs = [math.log(x) for _, x in points]
    t_mean, l_mean = sum(ts) / len(ts), sum(logs) / len(logs)
    b = (sum((t - t_mean) * (l - l_mean) for t, l in zip(ts, logs))
         / sum((t - t_mean) ** 2 for t in ts))
    a = l_mean - b * t_mean
    return math.exp(a), math.exp(b)

# Illustrative data: training compute of the largest model each year, in arbitrary units.
data = [(0, 1.0), (1, 4.6), (2, 19.0), (3, 95.0), (4, 410.0)]
x0, g = fit_exponential(data)
print(f"fitted growth: {g:.2f}x per year, doubling every {12 * doubling_time(g):.1f} months")
print(f"naive projection for year 8: {project(x0, g, 8):,.0f} units")

print(f"cost fell 280x in 1.92 years -> {280 ** (1 / 1.92):.1f}x cheaper per year")
print(f"50 minutes to 167 hours at 7-month doubling: {7 * periods_until(50, 167 * 60, 2):.0f} months")
```

Reading the code:

- **`doubling_time` takes a factor, not a percentage.** A growth of 40 percent per year is a factor of 1.4. Passing 40 would be a silent error that yields a plausible-looking wrong answer. Stating units in the docstring is a small habit that prevents it.
- **`periods_until` is `doubling_time` generalised.** Doubling is the special case where the target is twice the starting value. Both come from taking logarithms of the growth formula.
- **`fit_exponential` works on logarithms.** `math.log(x)` converts the curve into a straight line, and the next four lines are the textbook least-squares formulas. The function returns $x_0$ and $g$ in the original units by exponentiating the fitted intercept and slope. It needs every value to be positive, since the logarithm of zero or a negative number is undefined. Measure time from a baseline, as the sample data does with years 0 to 4. If you pass calendar years such as 2020, the growth factor is unaffected, but $x_0$ becomes the value extrapolated back two thousand years and underflows to zero.
- **The fit on the sample data gives about 4.5 times per year.** That is a doubling time of roughly five and a half months, matching the reported range for training compute.
- **The "naive projection" is labelled naive on purpose.** Extending the line four more years multiplies the last data point by several hundred. The code will happily print the number. Whether the world can supply the chips, electricity, and money is a question the formula cannot see.

That last point deserves a demonstration. The following adds a logistic curve and compares it with an exponential fitted to its early part.

```python
def logistic(t, ceiling, rate, midpoint):
    return ceiling / (1 + math.exp(-rate * (t - midpoint)))

truth = [(t, logistic(t, ceiling=1000, rate=1.5, midpoint=6)) for t in range(11)]
early = truth[:5]                                   # we only observe years 0 to 4
x0, g = fit_exponential(early)

print(" year   truth   exponential forecast")
for t, actual in truth:
    print(f"{t:5d} {actual:8.1f} {project(x0, g, t):12.1f}")
```

Run it and look at the table. For years 0 to 4 the exponential forecast tracks the true values closely. By year 6 the forecast is nearly double the truth. By year 10 it is hundreds of times too high, while the real quantity has flattened just under its ceiling of 1,000. The fit to the early data was excellent, and that tells you nothing about the forecast's quality, because an exponential and the early part of an S-curve are almost indistinguishable. This is the single most important lesson about technology forecasting, and it cuts both ways: people have also wrongly declared a ceiling many times while growth continued.

**Complexity.** Every function is constant-time except the fit, which makes a few passes over the $n$ data points and is therefore linear in $n$.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

**Check which curve a claim is about.** "AI is getting ten times cheaper every year" may refer to the price of a fixed capability, the price of the best model, or the cost of training. These move at very different rates, and the price of the frontier model has stayed roughly flat while what it can do has risen. Ask what exactly is being held constant.

**Cheaper per token does not mean a smaller bill.** Reasoning models and agents consume many times more tokens per task than a simple chat. Teams that budgeted on falling prices have been surprised by rising totals. Plan on cost per completed task, and measure it.

**"Should we wait for better models?" is usually the wrong question.** Models will improve. The work of understanding a process, cleaning its data, defining success, and building evaluation is the slow part, and it carries over to the next model unchanged. People who build things consistently advise starting now with a design that makes the model easy to swap.

**Build for the model that will exist when you ship.** A related habit: if something almost works today, it will probably work within a year, so do not over-invest in elaborate workarounds for a current model's weakness. Design them to be removable.

**Benchmarks saturate and get gamed.** A chart of a benchmark rising toward 100 percent shows progress on that benchmark. As Module 1.8 explained, contamination and tuning inflate scores, and a saturated benchmark stops measuring anything. Extrapolating a benchmark curve is weaker evidence than it looks.

**Exposure is not unemployment.** Headlines regularly convert "40 percent of jobs are exposed" into "40 percent of jobs will be lost". The underlying studies say tasks within jobs could be affected, in ways that may help or harm the worker. Read the definition before the number.

**Regulatory dates are moving targets.** Articles written a year ago may state compliance deadlines that have since been postponed. For anything a client will rely on, go to the primary legal source or a dated law-firm analysis and check the date on it.

**Forecasts say as much about the forecaster as about the future.** Company leaders raising money, critics selling books, and researchers seeking funding all have incentives. Prefer sources that publish their data and methods, state their uncertainty, and have a record you can check.

**Physical limits are real constraints.** Data-centre construction, chip supply, and electricity availability are now discussed as binding constraints on how fast compute can grow. These are the kinds of limit that bend an exponential into an S-curve.

**What does not change.** Whatever the frontier does, the fundamentals from this unit hold: models predict tokens, they have context limits, they can be wrong with confidence, and they need verification proportional to the stakes. Skills built on those fundamentals age well. Skills built on the quirks of one model version do not.

## 5. Active Recall Check & Synthesis Summary

**Summary.** AI progress has been driven by three compounding trends: training compute growing several-fold per year, steady algorithmic improvement, and a rapidly falling cost for any fixed level of capability. Growth is described by $x(t) = x_0 g^{t}$, with doubling time $\ln 2 / \ln g$, and trends are estimated by fitting a straight line to the logarithm of the data. Such fits describe the past well and cannot reveal when growth will slow, because an exponential and the early part of an S-curve look the same. Visible near-term directions are agents, reasoning at the time of use, multimodality, and on-device models. Economic effects are best understood as changes to tasks within jobs, with exposure distinct from displacement. Regulation such as the EU AI Act sorts systems by risk, and safety is increasingly a matter of engineering practice: test, monitor, control, and improve.

**Question 1.** Training compute for frontier models is reported to grow about 4 times per year. Compute the doubling time, and the total growth factor over five years if the trend continued. Then give one reason to doubt that it will continue at that rate.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

The doubling time is the natural log of 2 divided by the natural log of 4, which is 0.693 divided by 1.386, or 0.5 years: six months. Over five years the factor is 4 to the power 5, which is 1,024. A thousand-fold increase in five years would require a comparable increase in spending, chips, and electricity, since hardware efficiency improves far more slowly, with chip density doubling roughly every two years. Much of the past growth came from spending more, and budgets, chip manufacturing capacity, and power supply cannot expand a thousand-fold in five years. Those physical and financial limits are what would turn the exponential into an S-curve.

**Question 2.** A consultant shows a chart in which an exponential curve fits five years of data almost perfectly, and uses it to forecast the next five years. Explain why the quality of the fit says little about the forecast, using what the walkthrough demonstrated.

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

The early portion of a logistic S-curve grows almost exactly exponentially, so data generated by a process that will saturate fits an exponential just as well as data from a process that will keep growing. In the walkthrough, an exponential fitted to the first five points of a logistic curve matched them closely, yet overshot the true value by nearly a factor of two at the midpoint and by hundreds of times a few years later. A good fit confirms that the past was consistent with exponential growth. It contains no information about whether or when a limit will be reached. A credible forecast has to argue separately about the limits: resources, physics, demand, or data.

</details>

**Question 3.** A report states that 60 percent of jobs in advanced economies are "exposed to AI". A colleague concludes that most of these jobs will disappear. What is wrong with the inference, and how would you assess the effect on a specific role?

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

Exposure means that AI could perform or assist with a meaningful share of the tasks in a job. It does not specify the outcome. The same study that produced the figure judged that about half of the exposed jobs could see productivity gains while the other half face reduced labour demand, and historically technology has automated tasks more often than entire occupations. To assess a specific role, break it into its tasks, estimate which can be done reliably by current systems, which need human judgement, accountability, or physical presence, and how the time freed might be used. Then consider whether demand for the role's output will grow as it becomes cheaper. The result is a picture of how the role changes, which is more useful than a yes-or-no prediction about whether it survives.

A "growth factor" is what a quantity is MULTIPLIED by each period: 4 means it quadruples,

import math


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 1
def doubling_time(growth_factor):
    """From g ** T = 2. Takes a factor (1.4), never a percentage (40)."""
    return math.log(2) / math.log(growth_factor)


def growth_from_doubling(periods_to_double):
    """The inverse of doubling_time."""
    return 2 ** (1 / periods_to_double)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 2
def project(value, growth_factor, periods):
    """Compound growth. This is the formula that turns a trend into a headline."""
    return value * growth_factor ** periods


def periods_until(value, target, growth_factor):
    """Solve value * g ** t = target for t. Doubling time is the case target = 2 * value."""
    return math.log(target / value) / math.log(growth_factor)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 3
def annual_factor(start, end, years):
    """Annualise a change. A result below 1 means the quantity is shrinking."""
    return (end / start) ** (1 / years)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 4
def fit_exponential(points):
    """An exponential is a straight line in log space, so fit a line to (t, ln x).

    A good fit describes the past. It cannot tell you when growth will slow.
    Measure t from a baseline (0, 1, 2, ...), not as calendar years: with t = 2020 the
    growth factor is still right, but x0 is the value extrapolated back to "year 0"
    and underflows to 0.0.
    """
    ts = [t for t, _ in points]
    logs = [math.log(x) for _, x in points]
    t_mean = sum(ts) / len(ts)
    l_mean = sum(logs) / len(logs)
    b = (sum((t - t_mean) * (l - l_mean) for t, l in zip(ts, logs))
         / sum((t - t_mean) ** 2 for t in ts))
    a = l_mean - b * t_mean
    return math.exp(a), math.exp(b)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 5
def logistic(t, ceiling, rate, midpoint):
    """Growth with a limit. Early on it is almost indistinguishable from an exponential."""
    return ceiling / (1 + math.exp(-rate * (t - midpoint)))
