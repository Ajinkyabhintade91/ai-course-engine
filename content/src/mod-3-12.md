# Gemini Deep Research

Ask a chat assistant a research question and you get an answer in seconds, from memory and perhaps one or two searches. **Deep Research** is a different mode of work. You give it a question. It drafts a plan, spends several minutes searching and reading dozens of pages, and returns a structured report of several pages with citations.

Module 2.7 covered deep research in ChatGPT, and the general idea is the same across vendors. This chapter concentrates on what is particular to Gemini's version: a research plan you can edit before the work starts, the ability to research your own mail and files alongside the web, and export into Google's document tools. It then turns to the question that matters for any such tool, which is how to check what it gives you.

Details are from Google's pages as of November 2026.

## 1. First-Principles Mental Model & Intuition

### A research assistant who shows you the plan first

Imagine handing a question to a capable junior researcher. A good one does not vanish for a week and return with a report on the wrong thing. They come back in five minutes with a plan: "I will look at these four aspects, in this order, using these kinds of sources. Is that what you want?" You adjust it. Then they go away, read widely, and return with a memo and a list of references.

Gemini Deep Research follows that sequence.

```text
   YOU: a question
        |
        v
   1. PLAN        Gemini drafts a multi-step research plan
        |
        v
   2. REVIEW      you edit the plan, or approve it        <-- your best point of control
        |
        v
   3. RESEARCH    it searches, reads, and reasons for several minutes;
        |         you can leave and be notified when it finishes
        v
   4. REPORT      a structured, cited report
        |
        v
   5. FOLLOW UP   ask questions, request changes, export, or turn it into other formats
```

Two points about this picture shape how to use the tool.

**The plan is a contract.** A run takes minutes and counts against a limited allowance. A wrong plan produces a long, confident report about the wrong thing. Thirty seconds spent editing the plan is the cheapest correction you will ever make.

**The citations are leads.** A reference at the end of a sentence tells you where the assistant looked. It does not establish that the page says what the sentence says. The second half of this chapter is about that gap.

### What is being automated

Underneath, Deep Research is the orchestrator pattern from Module 3.9, run as a product. One process plans, sends out many searches, reads the results, notices gaps, searches again, and finally writes. Google describes it as a multi-step plan followed by searching, reading, reasoning over what was found, and producing a report, and says the same research system sits behind several of its products.

What you gain is breadth and time: thirty pages read in the time you would read two. What you do not gain is judgement about which of those pages deserved to be believed. That part stays with you.

### Steering the plan

A plan is a short list of research steps. Editing it is the main control you have, and four kinds of edit cover nearly everything.

| Edit | When | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Add** a step | Something you care about is missing | "Also compare the regulatory position in the EU" |
| **Remove** a step | A step is outside your question | Drop "history of the industry since 1990" |
| **Replace** a step | A step is too vague | "Look at costs" becomes "Find installed cost per kilowatt for 2024 to 2026" |
| **Reorder** steps | The emphasis is wrong | Put the question you most need answered first |

Beyond the steps, three instructions improve almost any run. Name the kinds of source you trust ("prefer official statistics and peer-reviewed studies"). Give a date range ("published since January 2025"). And state what to do about gaps ("where data is unavailable, say so").

### How many sources is that, really?

A report may list sixty references. That number overstates the evidence in two ways.

First, the same page often appears under several addresses: with and without "www", with tracking codes appended, with a trailing slash. **Normalising** the addresses collapses those.

Second, many pages are copies of one original. Ten news sites reprinting one press release are one source, not ten. What matters is the number of **independent origins**.

Why does this matter? Suppose a particular fact is covered by any one independent source with probability $c$. With $k$ independent sources, the chance that at least one covers it is

$$
P(\text{covered}) = 1 - (1 - c)^{k}
$$

With $c = 0.2$:

| Independent sources $k$ | Chance the fact is covered |
|---|---|
| 8 | 0.832 |
| 10 | 0.893 |
| 30 | 0.999 |

Thirty truly independent sources make coverage nearly certain. Thirty links that reduce to eight origins give 83 percent. The reference count on the report is the upper bound. The number of origins is the figure to reason with.

### Judging a source

A research tool reads what the search returns, and what the search returns includes forum posts, marketing pages, and articles written to rank well in search results. A simple score makes the judgement explicit. Give each kind of source a base weight, and reduce it with age:

$$
\text{score} = b_{\text{kind}} \times 0.5^{\,\text{age} / h}
$$

where $b_{\text{kind}}$ is the weight for the kind of source, age is in years, and $h$ is a half-life, the age at which a source counts half as much. With a half-life of three years, a news article with weight 0.7 from three years ago scores 0.35. A source with no date at all is penalised, because you cannot tell how stale it is.

The weights are a judgement and depend on the subject. For medicine, a three-year-old study may be current. For the price of a software subscription, three months is old. The value of writing a score down is that it forces you to decide.

### The arithmetic of citation errors

Suppose a report has $n$ citations and each has, independently, probability $p$ of not supporting the statement it is attached to. The expected number of faulty citations is

$$
E[\text{faulty}] = n \cdot p
$$

and the chance that every one is sound is

$$
P(\text{all sound}) = (1 - p)^{n}
$$

For $n = 40$ and $p = 0.10$, four faulty citations are expected, and the chance that all forty are sound is $0.9^{40} = 0.015$. Even at $p = 0.05$ with sixty citations, three are expected to be faulty and the chance of a clean report is under 5 percent.

The values of $p$ here are illustrative. What published evidence exists suggests that errors are common. A study by the Tow Center for Digital Journalism in March 2025 tested eight AI search tools on 1,600 queries about news articles and found that more than 60 percent of answers were wrong about the source, with wide variation between tools. That study examined chat search tools and not deep research agents, and the tools have changed since. It still sets a reasonable prior: assume that some citations in any long report are faulty, and that you do not know which.

Two conclusions follow. A spot-check of three citations that all turn out fine tells you little about the other thirty-seven. And since you cannot check everything, you must choose: identify the handful of claims your decision rests on, and check those at the source.

### What goes wrong

Published assessments and users' reports identify a consistent set of weaknesses in deep research tools.

- **The cited page does not support the claim.** The page exists and is on the topic, and the specific statement or number is not in it.
- **Invented or broken references.** Less common than it was, and still reported.
- **Weak sources given equal weight.** A forum comment and an official statistic appear side by side in the same confident prose.
- **Paywalled and offline material is missed.** The tool reads what is openly available, which skews toward free content.
- **Dates are missing or wrong.** An old figure is presented as current.
- **Length without substance.** Reports can be padded. Compare the report against the questions in your plan.
- **An answer to an unanswerable question.** Given a question with no good evidence, these tools tend to produce a confident report anyway.

### Gemini's version among the others

All the major assistants now offer a research mode. They differ in emphasis more than in kind, and the details change often.

| Tool | A distinguishing emphasis, as reported in this course |
|---|---|
| Gemini Deep Research | An editable plan before the run. Your Gmail, Drive, and notebooks as sources. Export to Google Docs |
| ChatGPT deep research (Module 2.7) | Clarifying questions before starting |

### Complexity

Editing a plan and normalising addresses are linear in their inputs. A run itself costs minutes and a large number of tokens, and the cost of verifying a report is proportional to the number of claims you choose to check, which is why choosing them is the skill.

## 3. Production-Grade Implementation & Annotated Walkthrough

The steering and the auditing can both be made concrete. First, the plan and its edits.

```python
def edit_plan(plan, edits):
    steps = list(plan)

    def check(index):
        if not 0 <= index < len(steps):
            raise ValueError(f"no step at position {index}")

    for edit in edits:
        action = edit[0]
        if action == "add":
            steps.append(edit[1])
        elif action == "remove":
            check(edit[1])
            del steps[edit[1]]
        elif action == "replace":
            check(edit[1])
            steps[edit[1]] = edit[2]
        elif action == "move":
            check(edit[1])
            check(edit[2])
            steps.insert(edit[2], steps.pop(edit[1]))
        else:
            raise ValueError(f"unknown edit: {action}")
    return steps

PLAN = ["History of heat pumps since 1990", "Look at costs", "Government incentives", "Common installation problems"]
EDITS = [("remove", 0),
         ("replace", 0, "Installed cost per kilowatt in the UK, 2024 to 2026, from official statistics"),
         ("add", "Where data is unavailable, say so"),
         ("move", 2, 0)]
for number, step in enumerate(edit_plan(PLAN, EDITS), start=1):
    print(number, step)
```

- **The function works on a copy.** `list(plan)` leaves the original untouched, so you can compare before and after.
- **Each edit is a small tuple naming its action.** This is the structure of the four kinds of edit in Section 2. Edits are applied in order, and positions refer to the plan as it stands at that moment, which is why the replacement targets position 0 after the first step has been removed.
- **Bad positions and unknown actions raise errors.** An edit that silently did nothing would leave you believing the plan had changed.
- **The result is a sharper plan.** The history step is gone, "look at costs" has become a question with units, dates, and a preferred source, the step on installation problems has moved to the top, and a rule for gaps has been added.

Next, counting the sources that are really there.

```python
from urllib.parse import parse_qsl, urlencode, urlsplit

def normalise_url(url):
    parts = urlsplit(url.strip())
    host = parts.netloc.lower()
    if host.startswith("www."):
        host = host[4:]
    query = urlencode([(key, value) for key, value in parse_qsl(parts.query) if not key.lower().startswith("utm_")])
    return host + parts.path.rstrip("/") + ("?" + query if query else "")

LINKS = [
    "https://www.example.gov/stats/heat-pumps/",
    "http://example.gov/stats/heat-pumps?utm_source=newsletter",
    "https://news.example.com/story?id=42&utm_campaign=spring",
    "https://news.example.com/story?id=42",
    "https://blog.example.org/heat-pumps#comments",
    "https://Example.GOV/stats/heat-pumps",
]
pages = {normalise_url(link) for link in LINKS}
origins = {page.split("/")[0].split("?")[0] for page in pages}
print(len(LINKS), "links ->", len(pages), "distinct pages ->", len(origins), "sites")
print(sorted(pages))
print("coverage with 30, 10, and 8 independent sources:", [round(1 - 0.8 ** k, 3) for k in (30, 10, 8)])
```

- **`urlsplit` separates an address into its parts.** Working on the parts is safer than editing the text, where a careless replacement could change the path.
- **Four differences are treated as meaningless.** Upper or lower case in the site name, a leading "www", a trailing slash, and tracking parameters beginning with `utm_`. The scheme and the fragment after a hash sign are dropped as well.
- **Meaningful parameters are kept.** `id=42` identifies the article. Removing every parameter would merge different articles on the same site.
- **Six links are three pages.** The official statistics page appears three times in different disguises and the news story twice.
- **Distinct pages are still an overestimate.** This code cannot tell that two different sites carry the same press release. Counting origins by content takes a person, or a comparison of the texts.

Then, scoring sources and estimating citation risk.

```python
BASE = {"government": 1.0, "academic": 1.0, "news": 0.7, "company": 0.5, "blog": 0.3, "forum": 0.2}

def score_source(kind, year, current_year, half_life=3):
    base = BASE.get(kind, 0.2)
    if year is None:
        return round(base * 0.5, 3)
    age = max(0, current_year - year)
    return round(base * 0.5 ** (age / half_life), 3)

def citation_risk(citations, error_rate):
    return citations * error_rate, (1 - error_rate) ** citations

for kind, year in [("academic", 2026), ("news", 2023), ("blog", 2020), ("government", None), ("press-release", 2026)]:
    print(f"{kind:<14} {str(year):<5} -> {score_source(kind, year, 2026)}")
for citations, rate in [(40, 0.10), (60, 0.05)]:
    expected, all_sound = citation_risk(citations, rate)
    print(f"{citations} citations at {rate:.0%}: expect {expected:.0f} faulty, chance all are sound {all_sound:.3f}")
```

- **The weights are in one table.** They are a judgement, and should be argued about. Putting them in data makes the argument possible and the change easy.
- **An unknown kind gets the lowest weight.** A source you cannot classify has not earned trust. The press release, which is not in the table, scores 0.2.
- **An undated source is halved.** The government page with no date scores 0.5, below a current academic source, because its age cannot be judged.
- **`max(0, ...)` handles a date in the future.** A source dated next year, usually a mistake in the metadata, is treated as current and not rewarded.
- **The risk figures are sobering.** Forty citations at a 10 percent error rate give four expected faults and a 1.5 percent chance of none. A report that long is almost never clean.

Finally, a check on the figures.

```python
import re

NUMBER = re.compile(r"\d+(?:\.\d+)?")

def numbers_in(text):
    return set(NUMBER.findall(text.replace(",", "")))

def unsupported_claims(claims, sources):
    problems = []
    for index, (text, source_id) in enumerate(claims):
        if source_id not in sources:
            problems.append((index, "missing-source"))
        elif not numbers_in(text) <= numbers_in(sources[source_id]):
            problems.append((index, "number-mismatch"))
    return problems

SOURCES = {
    "s1": "The city's 2025 budget report records 4,200 new homes completed, against a target of 5,000.",
    "s2": "Survey of 1,200 residents: 62% support the plan, 25% oppose it.",
}
CLAIMS = [
    ("4,200 new homes were completed in 2025.", "s1"),
    ("The target was 5,500 homes.", "s1"),
    ("62% of residents support the plan.", "s2"),
    ("Support rose by 8 points in a year.", "s3"),
    ("A quarter of residents oppose it.", "s2"),
]
print(unsupported_claims(CLAIMS, SOURCES))
```

- **Numbers are the easiest part of a claim to verify.** A figure either appears in the cited source or it does not. Commas are removed first so that "4,200" and "4200" match.
- **The subset test does the work.** `numbers_in(text) <= numbers_in(source)` asks whether every number in the claim appears in the source.
- **Claim 1, counting from 0, is caught.** The report says 5,500 and the source says 5,000. This is the commonest serious error in research reports: a real source, a wrong figure.
- **Claim 3 cites a source that was never supplied.** That is reported separately, since a missing source needs finding, not comparing.
- **Claim 4 passes, and should not reassure you.** "A quarter" contains no digits, so there is nothing for this check to compare. It happens to be true. A claim such as "most residents oppose it" would pass in exactly the same way. A mechanical check narrows the work, and reading finishes it.

**Complexity.** `edit_plan` is linear in the number of edits. `normalise_url` is linear in the length of an address. `unsupported_claims` is linear in the total length of the claims and the sources they cite.

### A prompt for a research run

```text
QUESTION      One sentence. What decision will this inform?
SCOPE         Geography, period, and what is out of scope.
SOURCES       Kinds of source to prefer and to avoid. "Published since [date]."
              Whether to use my files and mail, and which.
STRUCTURE     The sections I want, in order.
EVIDENCE      "For every figure, give the source and its publication date.
               Where sources disagree, show both. Where data is unavailable, say so."
LENGTH        A target, so that the report is not padded.
```

Then read the plan it proposes against this brief, edit it, and only then start the run.

### An audit routine for the report

1. **List the load-bearing claims.** The five or so statements your conclusion depends on.
2. **Open the cited source for each.** Find the sentence or the number. If it is not there, the claim is unsupported, however plausible.
3. **Check the date and the kind of each of those sources.** An undated blog is not evidence for a current figure.
4. **Collapse duplicates.** How many independent origins support each claim?
5. **Ask a follow-up for the weak ones.** "Find a primary source for the figure in section 3."
6. **Record what you verified.** In the exported document, mark each checked claim, so that a reader knows which parts have been confirmed by a person.

## 4. Practitioner Wisdom & Edge Cases (Reddit / HN / Stack Overflow Synthesis)

The reports below come from published assessments of deep research tools and from users' write-ups.

**Starting the run without reading the plan.** The most avoidable waste. A plan that includes a broad history you did not want, or misses the comparison you did, will produce exactly that report, several minutes and one allowance later.

**The cited page does not say that.** Reviewers of Gemini's and other tools' reports find claims attached to pages that are on the topic and do not contain the claim. The citation looks like verification and is not. Open the page for anything you will repeat.

**Low-quality sources dressed in good prose.** Users report forum threads and thin, search-optimised articles cited alongside official data, with nothing in the writing to distinguish them. Say in the brief which kinds of source you want, and look at the reference list before you read the report.

**Missing dates.** Assessments note that sources are often cited with the date of access and no date of publication. A figure with no date cannot be called current. Ask for publication dates explicitly.

**Paywalled knowledge is invisible.** Academic journals, trade publications, and industry reports behind a paywall are largely absent. If the important literature in your field is not free, the report is working from what is left. Upload the key papers yourself.

**Flattering reports about companies.** Users observe a promotional tone when a report draws heavily on companies' own pages. A company's website is a source about what the company says, not about how good its product is.

**It answers questions that have no answer.** Asked about something for which little evidence exists, the tool still returns a full report. A good brief asks it to state where evidence is thin, and a good reader notices when twenty pages rest on three sources.

**Length is mistaken for depth.** A long report feels thorough. Check it against the questions in your plan. Sections that restate the question or summarise the obvious are padding.

**Internal sources leak through exports.** A report that drew on Gmail and Drive contains material from them. Exporting it to a document and sharing the link shares that material. Check what is in the report before it leaves you.

**Audio summaries simplify.** A spoken overview of a report is a pleasant way to absorb it and necessarily drops caveats. Do not treat it as a substitute for reading the sections you will act on.

**Limits are opaque.** The number of runs allowed is not published on the help page and has changed over time. Do not spend a run on a question a normal chat could answer.

**One tool's report is one opinion.** Different research tools, given the same question, choose different sources and sometimes reach different conclusions. For a consequential question, compare two.

## 5. Active Recall Check & Synthesis Summary

**Summary.** Gemini Deep Research turns a question into a plan, lets you edit that plan, spends several minutes searching and reading, and returns a cited report that can be exported to a document or transformed into other formats. Its particular strengths are the editable plan and the ability to use your own mail, files, and notebooks as sources beside the web. The plan is the cheapest point of control, steered by adding, removing, replacing, and reordering steps and by naming preferred sources and dates. A report's reference count overstates its evidence, because addresses repeat and pages copy one another, and coverage depends on independent origins as $1 - (1 - c)^k$. Citations are leads: with $n$ of them at error rate $p$, about $n \cdot p$ are faulty and the chance of a clean report is $(1 - p)^n$, which is small for any long report. The practical response is to choose the few claims a decision rests on and verify those at their sources, with attention to figures, dates, and the kind of source.

**Question 1.** A report lists 50 citations. Assume each has an 8 percent chance of not supporting its claim. Compute the expected number of faulty citations and the chance that all 50 are sound. A colleague checks four citations at random, finds all four sound, and declares the report reliable. Using the same assumption, how likely was that result, and what does it show?

<details>
<summary>View Answer</summary>

The expected number of faulty citations is 50 times 0.08, which is 4. The chance that all fifty are sound is 0.92 to the fiftieth power, about 0.015, or one and a half percent. So under this assumption the report almost certainly contains several faulty citations. The colleague's result, four out of four sound, has probability 0.92 to the fourth power, about 0.716. It would be found about 72 percent of the time even though the report has four faulty citations in it. The check was therefore very likely to pass whether or not the report is reliable, which means that passing it is weak evidence. A random spot-check of a few citations can detect a report that is badly wrong throughout. It cannot detect a handful of errors among fifty. The effective approach is not to sample at random but to choose: identify the claims the conclusion depends on and verify each of those, since an error in a load-bearing claim matters and an error in a passing remark does not.

**Question 2.** A report's reference list has 36 links. After normalising the addresses there are 24 distinct pages. On inspection, those pages trace back to 9 independent origins, because many reprint the same two press releases. If any one independent source covers a given fact with probability 0.2, compute the chance the fact is covered using 36, 24, and 9 as the number of sources. Which figure should you use, and what does the difference imply for how you brief the tool?

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 1
def edit_plan(plan, edits):
    """Work on a copy, apply edits in order, and fail loudly on a bad position or action:
    an edit that silently did nothing would leave you believing the plan had changed."""
    steps = list(plan)

    def check(index):
        if not 0 <= index < len(steps):
            raise ValueError(f"no step at position {index}")

    for edit in edits:
        action = edit[0]
        if action == "add":
            steps.append(edit[1])
        elif action == "remove":
            check(edit[1])
            del steps[edit[1]]
        elif action == "replace":
            check(edit[1])
            steps[edit[1]] = edit[2]
        elif action == "move":
            check(edit[1])
            check(edit[2])
            steps.insert(edit[2], steps.pop(edit[1]))
        else:
            raise ValueError(f"unknown edit: {action}")
    return steps


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 2
def normalise_url(url):
    """Case, "www", a trailing slash, tracking parameters, the scheme, and the fragment
    do not change which page an address points to. Meaningful parameters are kept."""
    parts = urlsplit(url.strip())
    host = parts.netloc.lower()
    if host.startswith("www."):
        host = host[4:]
    query = urlencode([(key, value) for key, value in parse_qsl(parts.query) if not key.lower().startswith("utm_")])
    return host + parts.path.rstrip("/") + ("?" + query if query else "")


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 3
def score_source(kind, year, current_year, half_life=3):
    """Weights are a judgement kept in one table. An unknown kind gets the lowest weight,
    and an undated source is halved because its age cannot be judged."""
    base = BASE.get(kind, 0.2)
    if year is None:
        return round(base * 0.5, 3)
    age = max(0, current_year - year)
    return round(base * 0.5 ** (age / half_life), 3)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 4
def citation_risk(citations, error_rate):
    """For any long report the chance that every citation is sound is small."""
    return citations * error_rate, (1 - error_rate) ** citations


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- Step 5
def unsupported_claims(claims, sources):
    """A figure either appears in the cited source or it does not. A claim with no digits
    passes this check without being verified: reading finishes what the check narrows."""
    problems = []
    for index, (text, source_id) in enumerate(claims):

            problems.append((index, "missing-source"))
        elif not numbers_in(text) <= numbers_in(sources[source_id]):
            problems.append((index, "number-mismatch"))
    return problems
